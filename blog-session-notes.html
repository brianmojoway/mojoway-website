Coaching Notes: What to Write, What to Ignore, and a 90-Second Review

2026-10-12 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

You finish a session, say goodbye, and glance at the clock. The next client is in ten minutes. You have just enough time to grab water, stretch, and maybe scribble a few words about what just happened. But what words? The wrong notes become a transcript you never revisit. The right notes become a quiet companion that helps you coach better, session after session.

This article is about a simple, sustainable approach to coaching notes. It is not about capturing every detail, and it is not about building a perfect archive. It is about writing down what actually helps you show up with more presence, more curiosity, and more continuity for your client. You will learn what to record, what to ignore, and how to review your notes in ninety seconds before the next session.

What actually helps you coach better

Think about the last time you felt truly prepared for a session. You probably were not holding a detailed script. You were holding a few key threads: what the client is working toward, what shifted last time, and what you are curious about now. That is the level of detail your notes should serve. They are not a legal record. They are a thinking tool for you.

For example, one coach I know used to write pages of dialogue. After a few months, she realized she never read them. She switched to three lines per session: the client's stated focus, one moment of energy or resistance, and one question she wanted to hold. Her preparation time dropped, and her sessions felt more connected. The notes were doing their job: helping her coach, not helping her archive.

  • The client's own words for what they want from coaching
  • A shift in energy, tone, or language you noticed
  • One question or theme you want to return to
  • Any commitment the client made, in their words
  • What you, the coach, felt curious about but did not pursue

What to leave out (and why less is more)

It is tempting to write down everything: the client's backstory, your interpretations, the clever intervention you tried. But notes that are too dense become a burden. You avoid reviewing them because they take too long. You start treating them as a case file rather than a live map. The result is less presence, not more.

Leave out your judgments about the client's choices. Leave out long summaries of past sessions that you can reconstruct from memory or from the client's own recap. Leave out anything that feels like a diagnosis or a fixed conclusion. Coaching notes should stay provisional. They are your observations, not a verdict.

One coach described her old notes as 'a novel I never wanted to reread.' She cut them down to a few bullet points and found she actually used them. The test is simple: if a note does not help you be more curious or more useful in the next session, it probably does not belong.

A simple session notes template you can use today

You do not need a fancy app. A notebook, a notes file, or a simple document works. The structure matters more than the tool. Try this four-part template, written immediately after the session while it is still fresh. It takes two to three minutes.

First, write the client's focus in their own words. Second, note one moment that stood out: a pause, a laugh, a change in pace, a repeated phrase. Third, write one thing you want to remember or follow up on. Fourth, write one question you are holding for next time. That is it. Four lines, maximum.

For example, after a session with a client who wants to delegate more, your notes might read: 'Focus: stop being the bottleneck. Noticed: paused when I asked about her team lead. Follow up: she mentioned a conversation she is avoiding. Question: what does she fear will happen if she lets go?' That is enough to walk into the next session with continuity and curiosity.

  • Focus: the client's goal in their own words
  • Noticed: one specific moment or shift
  • Follow up: one thing to remember or revisit
  • Question: one thing you are curious about

The ninety-second review before your next session

The review is where notes earn their keep. Ninety seconds is enough. Set a timer if it helps. Open your notes from the last session and read only the four lines you wrote. Do not read older entries unless you need context. The goal is not to memorize. The goal is to arrive with a thread to pick up.

As you read, ask yourself three quiet questions: What was alive for this client last time? What did I notice that I did not name? What am I curious about now? Then close the notes. Take one breath. You are ready. This small ritual does more for your coaching than an hour of re-reading ever would.

One executive coach I spoke with does this between every session. She keeps a single index card per client. Before each call, she reads the card for ninety seconds, then puts it away. She says it helps her avoid the two most common traps: starting from scratch every time, and clinging to an old plan that no longer fits.

Making the habit stick without adding admin

The best note-taking system is the one you will actually use. If you try to write during the session, you may lose presence. If you wait until the end of the day, you may lose detail. The sweet spot is a brief capture right after the session, while the client's words are still in your ears.

Keep it to two or three minutes. Use the same four prompts every time so you do not have to think about structure. Store notes where you will see them before the next session, not in a folder you never open. And review for ninety seconds, not ninety minutes.

Over time, these notes become a record of growth, not just a to-do list. They show you patterns, themes, and shifts you might otherwise miss. They help you coach with more continuity and more humanity. And they take less time than brewing a cup of tea.
