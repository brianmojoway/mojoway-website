How to Show Coaching Progress When the Client Feels Stuck

2026-10-13 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

You have a client who feels stuck. They say nothing has changed. But you see it: the way they pause before reacting, the new question they ask themselves, the small boundary they held last week. You could argue, point out evidence, list the wins. That usually backfires. The client digs in, feels unseen, and the session becomes a debate about reality instead of a space for reflection.

The problem is not that progress is invisible. It is that progress is invisible to the person living it. From the inside, change feels like the same old story with slightly different weather. Your job is not to convince. Your job is to make the movement visible in a way the client can hold, touch, and recognise as their own. Two simple artefacts do that better than any argument.

Why arguing about progress fails

When a client says 'I am stuck,' they are not making a factual claim. They are describing a felt sense. Arguing with that sense, even gently, puts you on opposite sides. The more evidence you offer, the more they scan for counter-evidence. This is not stubbornness. It is how attention works when someone feels unheard.

A coach who argues progress becomes a cheerleader. Cheerleading creates dependency. The client waits for you to tell them they are doing well. That is the opposite of what coaching is for. Instead, shift the frame: do not tell them they have moved. Show them a record of their own words and actions, and let them draw the conclusion.

Artefact one: the five-element check-in log

The first artefact is a simple log the client keeps between sessions. It is not a journal. It is a five-line check-in based on the MOJOWAY five elements: Wood (vision, direction), Fire (energy, connection), Earth (grounding, routine), Metal (clarity, boundaries), Water (rest, reflection). Each day or week, the client rates each element from 1 to 5 and writes one sentence about what they noticed.

The power is in the pattern, not the numbers. After three or four weeks, you do not interpret the log. You ask the client to read it aloud. They see their own words: 'I said no to the extra project' (Metal). 'I slept badly but still walked' (Water and Earth). 'I felt excited about the new idea' (Fire and Wood). The client is the one who notices the movement. You just hold the space.

This artefact works because it is concrete and owned by the client. It does not require you to prove anything. It also gives you a shared language for the session. Instead of 'How are you?' you can ask 'What did you notice in your Metal line this week?' That question is specific, and it invites reflection rather than defence.

  • Keep it to five lines and one sentence per element. More than that becomes a chore.
  • Do not grade or interpret the ratings. Ask the client what they see.
  • Use the log at the start of a session to set the focus, not to review every entry.

Artefact two: the before-and-after question card

The second artefact is a single card, physical or digital, with two columns. On the left, the client writes the question they were asking themselves at the start of coaching. On the right, they write the question they are asking now. For example, left: 'How do I survive this job?' Right: 'What would I need to change to feel curious again?' The questions are different. The client sees that their own inquiry has shifted.

You introduce this artefact in session three or four, not at the start. Ask the client to recall a recent moment when they handled something differently. Then ask: what question were you asking yourself in that moment? Write it down. Compare it to the question they brought to coaching. The gap between the two is the progress. You do not have to name it. They do.

This works because questions reveal identity. A stuck person asks survival questions. A moving person asks possibility questions. When the client sees their own question change, they cannot easily dismiss it as your opinion. It is their handwriting, their words, their shift.

How to introduce the artefacts without pressure

Do not present these as homework or as a measurement tool. Present them as experiments. Say: 'I would like to try something with you. It is not a test. It is a way for you to see your own patterns without me telling you what they mean.' Then show your own example, briefly. A coach who keeps a five-element log themselves can speak from experience, not theory.

Timing matters. The check-in log works best after the client has expressed frustration about not knowing where they are. The question card works best after a small win they almost overlooked. In both cases, you are offering a container for what is already happening, not adding a task.

If the client resists, do not push. Say: 'That is fine. We can leave it. If you change your mind, the offer stands.' Resistance often softens when the pressure to perform disappears. The artefact is there when they are ready.

What to do when the client still feels stuck

Sometimes the artefacts show little movement. That is information, not failure. It may mean the goal is wrong, the pace is unrealistic, or something outside coaching needs attention. Your role is not to force progress. It is to help the client see clearly, including seeing when something is not working.

In that case, return to the five elements. Ask: which element feels most depleted? Which one feels most alive? Often the stuckness is localised. A client may feel stuck in their career (Wood) but alive in their relationships (Fire). Naming that difference reduces the global sense of failure. It also points to where the next small step might be.

Remember that MOJOWAY is a reflection tool, not a clinical instrument. It does not diagnose or treat anything. If a client is in real distress, coaching may not be the right support. Know your scope, and refer when needed. That boundary is part of ethical practice, and it protects the client.

The coach's own practice

You cannot show a client how to see movement if you do not practise seeing it in yourself. Keep your own five-element log for a month. Notice how your own sense of stuckness shifts when you read your own words. Use the question card on yourself. What question are you asking now that you were not asking a year ago?

This is not extra work. It is the core of the practice. When you speak from your own experience, you do not need to convince anyone. You simply offer the tools that have worked for you. That is the most honest form of coaching progress there is.
