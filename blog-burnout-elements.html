Burnout Coaching: Which Energy Is Actually Depleted?

2026-09-25 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

When a client says they are burned out, the instinct is to reach for a single explanation: too much work, too little sleep, a boss who does not listen. But in practice, burnout rarely arrives from one direction. It arrives when a particular kind of energy has been running on empty for too long, and the usual advice, rest more, do less, does not match the actual deficit.

MOJOWAY is a personal reflection framework built on the five elements, used by coaches to help clients notice patterns rather than diagnose problems. It is not a clinical tool and it does not tell you what is wrong with someone. What it does well is give a shared language for a simple question: which energy is depleted, and what would a small, honest refill look like this week?

Two ways energy goes missing: overuse and starvation

Depletion shows up in two very different shapes, and confusing them is why so much burnout support misses the mark. Overuse is when one energy is being spent constantly and never replenished. The client who is endlessly organizing, deciding, and holding the plan together is overusing the energy of structure and will. Starvation is the opposite: an energy that is simply not being fed at all. The client who has not had a genuinely playful hour in months is not overdoing play. They are starving it.

The distinction matters because the remedies pull in opposite directions. Overused energy needs boundaries, delegation, and deliberate pauses. Starved energy needs a small, regular input, not a rest. Telling a starved client to rest more often makes things worse, because rest does not feed what is missing. Telling an overused client to add one more nourishing practice can feel like one more item on the list.

A simple way to tell them apart: ask what the client does automatically, without thinking, even when tired. That is usually the overused energy. Then ask what they cannot remember the last time they did. That is usually the starved one.

Reading the five energies without turning it into a diagnosis

The five elements give us five flavours of energy to notice: the initiating, forward-moving energy of Wood; the warm, connecting energy of Fire; the steady, holding energy of Earth; the refining, letting-go energy of Metal; and the quiet, resting energy of Water. None is better than another. Each can be overused or starved, and most clients are doing both at once in different places.

The point is not to label the person. It is to locate the pattern. A client might be overusing Fire, saying yes to every conversation and carrying the emotional temperature of the whole team, while starving Water, never having a quiet hour that is not scheduled. That is a very different picture from a client overusing Wood, pushing hard on new initiatives, while starving Earth, with no routine that holds them steady.

Keep the language plain and tentative. I notice you seem to be running on connection all day, and there is not much quiet in your week. Does that land? The client corrects you if it does not, and the correction is often more useful than the original guess.

  • Wood: starting, deciding, pushing forward. Overused as relentless drive; starved as no sense of direction.
  • Fire: connecting, warming, being seen. Overused as constant availability; starved as loneliness in a crowded week.
  • Earth: holding, steadying, caring for the practical. Overused as carrying everyone; starved as no routine that supports you.
  • Metal: refining, discerning, letting go. Overused as harsh self-critique; starved as clutter and no clear standards.
  • Water: resting, reflecting, restoring. Overused as withdrawal; starved as no genuine downtime.

A worked example: the manager who could not stop deciding

A client, a operations manager in her forties, came with classic burnout language: flat, irritable, sleeping badly, dreading Mondays. The obvious read was overwork. But when we looked at the week, the hours were not extreme. What was extreme was the number of decisions she made. Every small choice, from staffing to supplier emails to what her team should prioritize, came through her.

That is overused Wood: the energy of initiating and deciding, running all day with no pause. Her starved energy was Water, the quiet, reflective register. She had no unscheduled time at all. Even her evenings were planned. The refill was not a holiday. It was twenty minutes, three times a week, with no input and no decisions, and a rule that two small decisions per day would be handed to someone else.

The first two weeks felt wrong to her. She reported feeling guilty and slightly bored. By week four she described the twenty minutes as the only part of the day that felt like hers. Nothing dramatic happened. The point is not that this fixed her burnout. It is that the refill matched the actual deficit, which is why it was sustainable when grander plans were not.

The small refill that actually helps

When coaches ask what refill to suggest, the temptation is to go big: a retreat, a sabbatical, a complete restructure. Big moves are hard to start and harder to keep, and they often address the wrong energy anyway. The refill that helps is usually small, specific, and repeated. It is small enough that a tired person will actually do it, and specific enough that it feeds the starved energy rather than just adding comfort.

Match the refill to the deficit. For starved Water, it might be a walk with no podcast. For starved Fire, a twenty-minute call with someone who knows you well and does not need anything from you. For starved Earth, one repeating anchor in the week, same time, same place, low effort. For starved Metal, ten minutes of tidying one drawer or one inbox folder. For starved Wood, one small thing started and finished in an afternoon.

Keep it to one refill per client, not five. The instinct to offer a menu is understandable, but a menu becomes another task. One refill, done three times, tells you more than five refills done once.

  • Ask: what did you used to do that you have stopped doing? That is often the starved energy.
  • Make it small enough to survive a bad week.
  • Anchor it to something that already happens, like after the school run or before the first meeting.
  • Review it after two weeks. If it did not happen, the refill was too big, not the client too weak.

What the coach does with this

This is reflection, not treatment. The coach is not assessing or fixing a condition. The coach is holding a mirror steady while the client notices their own pattern. That means asking more than telling, and being willing to be wrong. If the client says the Wood reading does not fit, drop it and look again.

The most useful thing a coach can offer is the distinction between overuse and starvation, because it reframes the whole conversation. Instead of how do I recover from burnout, the question becomes which energy have I been spending without refilling, and which have I stopped feeding altogether. That is a question a client can answer for themselves, and it usually points somewhere more honest than generic rest advice.

Keep the scope clear. If a client describes symptoms that concern you, refer them to a qualified professional. The five energies are a way to talk about patterns in a coaching conversation, not a health assessment, and they work best when everyone is clear about that.
