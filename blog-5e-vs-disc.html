Five Energies vs DISC: What Each One Actually Tells a Coach

2026-11-08 · 7 min read · by Brian Zhang

A coach usually meets a DISC profile before they meet the person. It arrives in an email attachment from HR, four letters and a colour grid, already flattened into something a manager can read in thirty seconds. It is useful. It is also a description of the surface: how this person tends to act when a deadline is near, how they speak in a meeting, how they handle a disagreement in a corridor.

The five energies answer a different question. Not what does this person do, but what are they organising their life around. That difference sounds small until you sit with a client who scores high on steadiness and quietly describes a decade of saying yes to work they did not want. DISC names the behaviour. The energies give you somewhere to start asking why it holds. This is a comparison of two tools that are often treated as rivals and are not really competing.

What DISC was built to do

DISC sorts behaviour along two broad axes: pace and assertiveness on one, and task focus versus people focus on the other. Out of that come four familiar styles. It was designed to be quick to administer, easy to explain and safe to use in a team setting. A facilitator can hand a room of twenty people their profiles and have a shared vocabulary by lunchtime.

That speed is the point. It is also the limit. DISC describes a pattern of observable behaviour in a work context, and it deliberately stays there. It does not ask what a person is protecting, what they are hungry for, or why a style that looks calm on paper feels exhausting to live inside. Ask it about motivation and it has nothing to say, because motivation was never what it was measuring.

For a coach, the practical consequence is that DISC is excellent for the first ten minutes of a conversation and thin after that. It gives you a shared language. It does not give you a question worth sitting with.

What the five energies were built to do

The five energies are a lens for conversation, not a fixed personality type. Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water each name a way of moving through the world: growth and direction, connection and visibility, steadiness and care, precision and standards, depth and withdrawal. A reading gives you a dominant tendency and, more usefully, the shape of what that tendency costs when it is overused.

It is built from a birth date and, if the person has them, a small set of body-composition numbers. That sounds unusual to anyone raised on questionnaires, and it is worth being plain about it: the framework is a reflection tool. It is not a clinical or diagnostic instrument, and it is not a psychometric test with a normative sample. What it produces is a starting map for a conversation, not a verdict about a person.

The output a coach actually receives is deliberately small. Three topics the client is likely to be circling, two angles to open them from, and one blind spot the client probably cannot see from where they are standing. That is enough to prepare a first session and not so much that you walk in believing you already know them.

Where the two lenses disagree

The interesting moments are where they point in different directions. A client can present as steady, agreeable and reliable in a DISC sense while describing a life organised around an enormous private ambition. Behaviour says Earth; appetite says Wood. If you only have the behaviour, you will build a session around boundaries and rest, and the client will politely agree with you and never come back.

The reverse happens too. Someone who reads as forceful and fast in a team can be running on a deep need for quiet and a long stretch of time alone. Push on performance and you will get a competent answer. Ask about what they are refilling and the conversation changes register completely.

This is the strongest argument for holding both lightly. A behaviour profile tells you how to pitch the room. An energy read tells you which door might actually be open. Neither is a licence to tell a client who they are.

  • DISC: how a person tends to behave, especially at work; fast to share, easy to misuse as a label
  • Five energies: what a person may be organising around, and what that costs; slower, better as a question than an answer
  • Best use: one for the language of the conversation, one for choosing where to begin

How to use both in a first session

Start with what the client brought. If HR sent a DISC profile, read it before the session and then set it aside. Use its vocabulary only when the client uses it themselves. Nothing shuts a conversation down faster than a coach naming a quadrant before the person has finished describing their own week.

Bring the energy read as a hypothesis, not a finding. A workable opening is: I have a sense that rest is not the issue here, that something has been waiting for a decision for a while. Does that land, or am I reading it wrong. The second half of that sentence matters more than the first. It gives the client room to correct you, and their correction is usually the real material of the session.

Keep the three topics and the blind spot in your notes rather than on the table. Coaches who hand over the whole read in the first twenty minutes tend to get agreement instead of discovery. The point of preparation is that you can afford to listen, because you already know the three places you might need to return to.

When to reach for which one

If the work is team dynamics, feedback language or helping a manager and a report stop talking past each other, DISC is the better instrument. It is shared, neutral and quick. If the work is a person deciding what to do next, or noticing a pattern they keep repeating, the five energies give you more to work with.

If you are running an intake before session one and have ten minutes of the client's time, a short set of questions plus an energy read will usually produce a more useful first meeting than a completed behaviour grid. The grid tells you how they will sit in the room. The read suggests what they have not yet said out loud.

Most coaches end up with both on the shelf. That is fine. The mistake is not choosing the wrong one; it is treating either as the answer instead of the opening.
