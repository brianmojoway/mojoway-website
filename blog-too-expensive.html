When a Client Says 'Too Expensive': What to Ask Next

2026-09-27 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

A client goes quiet on the call, then says it: 'It's just too expensive.' Most coaches hear a number problem and start defending the number. They list what's included, mention payment plans, or knock a little off the top. Sometimes that works. Often it doesn't, because the number was never the whole story.

In MOJOWAY, the Five Elements give you a way to listen past the sentence itself. A price objection is information. It tells you something about where the client is standing, what they're protecting, and whether they trust you enough to spend real money on the work. Your next question matters more than your next discount.

Start with the question under the question

When someone says 'too expensive,' they usually mean one of three things: I don't see the value yet, I can't access the money right now, or I don't trust this enough to risk it. Those are very different problems. A payment plan solves the second. Curiosity and proof solve the first. Only time, honesty, and consistency solve the third.

So the first move is not to answer. It's to ask. Try something simple and unhurried: 'When you say expensive, expensive compared to what?' This gives the client room to tell you what they're measuring against. Maybe it's another coach. Maybe it's their rent. Maybe it's a previous program that didn't deliver. Whatever they say next is more useful than anything you could have scripted.

A worked example. A coach I'll call Dana is on a discovery call with a marketing director in Manchester. The fee is 3,200 pounds for three months. The client says, 'That's more than I expected.' Dana does not drop the price. She asks, 'More than you expected, or more than feels safe right now?' The client pauses, then admits she was burned by a consultant last year who took the money and disappeared. The objection was never the 3,200. It was the memory of being let down.

  • Ask: 'Expensive compared to what?'
  • Ask: 'Is it the amount, the timing, or the risk?'
  • Ask: 'What would need to be true for this to feel like a clear yes?'
  • Then stop talking. Let the silence do the work.

What the energy read tells you that words don't

MOJOWAY is a reflection framework built on the Five Elements: Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water. It is not a diagnostic tool and it does not tell you what a client will do. It gives you a lens for noticing how someone is showing up in the conversation. That lens is useful here because price objections are rarely delivered with the same energy.

A Wood-dominant client often pushes back with forward motion. They want to know the plan, the milestones, the return. 'Too expensive' from this person usually means 'I can't see the path from here to the result.' They are not asking for a discount. They are asking for a map. Give them the sequence and the objection often softens on its own.

A Fire-dominant client may say the price is high while their face says they're already in. They want to be seen, encouraged, and connected. If they hesitate, it's often about whether they'll feel supported when it gets hard. The number is a proxy for 'will you be there?' A Metal-dominant client is different again. They want clarity, standards, and proof. Vagueness reads as risk to them, and risk reads as expensive.

Earth and Water: the quieter objections

Earth clients are steady and relational. They rarely lead with price. If they do, it usually means they're worried about disruption: to their family, their schedule, their sense of stability. 'Too expensive' can mean 'I can't picture how this fits into my life without something else falling apart.' The useful question here is about capacity, not cost. 'What would need to shift in your week for this to feel manageable?'

Water clients are the ones who go still. They may agree with everything you say and then mention the price very softly, almost apologetically. Water is about depth, timing, and trust. A Water client who objects to price is often saying 'I'm not sure I'm ready to be seen.' Pushing harder here backfires. Slowing down and naming what you notice works better. 'I get the sense something in you is weighing this carefully. What's coming up?'

A worked example. A leadership coach in Toronto is talking to a founder who has gone quiet. The fee is 6,000 dollars. The founder says, 'That's a lot.' The coach notices the stillness, the long pause, the careful wording. Instead of justifying the fee, she says, 'It sounds like part of you wants this and part of you is protecting something.' The founder exhales and says, 'I've never told anyone at work that I'm struggling.' The price was never the issue. The exposure was.

Match your next move to what you're hearing

Once you have a read, you can respond with something that fits instead of something that discounts. This is not about typing a client into a box. It's about choosing a response that meets the person in front of you. Most coaches default to one move, usually explaining more. That move only works for one kind of objection.

For a Wood client, give the path. For a Fire client, give the warmth and the commitment. For an Earth client, give the container and the practical fit. For a Metal client, give the standard and the evidence. For a Water client, give the space and the honesty. None of these require you to lower your fee. They require you to hear what's actually being asked.

And sometimes the answer is genuinely no. Some clients cannot afford you right now, and that is real. The point of this work is not to talk everyone into saying yes. It is to stop confusing a trust gap with a budget gap. When you can tell them apart, you stop discounting work that was never overpriced, and you stop chasing clients who were never going to feel safe saying yes.

  • Wood: show the route from here to the outcome.
  • Fire: show that you'll stay with them when it gets uncomfortable.
  • Earth: show how it fits into the life they already have.
  • Metal: show the standard, the structure, and the evidence.
  • Water: slow down, name what you notice, and let them choose.

What to do after the call

Write two lines in your notes before you do anything else. First, what they said about the price. Second, what you noticed in their energy. Over a few months, patterns show up. You may find that most of your price objections come from one or two Elements, and that tells you something about your own positioning and your own default response.

It also tells you something about you. Coaches who fear the price conversation tend to attract clients who mirror that fear. If you notice yourself rushing to justify, discounting before you're asked, or avoiding the topic entirely, that is worth reflecting on. The objection is a mirror as much as a message.

The next time a client says 'too expensive,' try this: thank them for saying it, ask what they're comparing it to, and listen for the Element underneath. You may still lose the client. But you will lose them for the right reason, and you will keep the ones who were always going to say yes.
