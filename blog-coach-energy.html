Your Energy as a Coaching Instrument: Noticing Drain

2026-10-21 · 6 min read · by Brian Zhang

You finish a session with one client and feel oddly light, as if you have more room in your chest than when you started. With another client, you finish and reach for your phone to scroll, not because you are curious but because you need a moment of nothing. Both responses are data. In a practice built on attention, your own energy is one of the most honest instruments you have, and it is usually the last one we think to read.

This is not about labeling clients as difficult or yourself as fragile. It is about treating your felt sense of depletion as information about fit, pacing, and the shape of the work. Coaches who last tend to be the ones who notice the signal early and respond with structure rather than guilt. Here is how to read the instrument, and what to do with what it tells you.

Notice the drain without making it a verdict

Start with simple observation. After each session, take thirty seconds before you open your calendar or your inbox. Ask: did that session give me energy, hold it steady, or take more than it gave? Write one word in your notes. Not a judgment of the client, not a story about your competence, just a word. Over three or four weeks, patterns appear that a single hard day cannot show you.

Consider a coach we will call Maya. She noticed that her Tuesday afternoon client, a senior operations director, left her feeling flattened every week. Her first instinct was to assume she was not skilled enough for the complexity. But when she looked at her notes, the sessions themselves were fine. The drain came from the twenty minutes after each one, when she replayed the conversation in her head, worried she had missed something, and drafted follow-up emails she never sent. The drain was not the client. It was the unresolved loop the client triggered in her.

  • Rate energy after each session with a single word: lighter, steady, heavier.
  • Note the time of day and what came before the session, not just the session itself.
  • Look for the loop: what you replay afterward is often the real source of depletion.

Ask what the drain is telling you about the work

Depletion is rarely about the person in front of you. It usually points to one of three things: a mismatch in the kind of work you are built for, a boundary that is quietly leaking, or a session structure that asks you to hold more than one person should. A coach who loves strategic clarity may find endless emotional processing exhausting, while another coach finds the reverse. Neither is a flaw. Both are fit.

The boundary leak is the most common. You agree to a session at 7pm because the client is in another time zone. You answer a message on a Sunday because it feels urgent. You extend a session by ten minutes because the conversation is raw. Each of these is small. Together they turn a practice into something that never quite closes. The drain you feel is often the cost of a door that does not shut.

  • Mismatch: the work asks for a muscle you do not enjoy using for long stretches.
  • Leak: a boundary you have not named, so the client cannot respect it.
  • Overload: a session structure that leaves you holding what should be shared or referred.

Set load limits before you need them

A load limit is a decision you make in calm weather so you do not have to make it in a storm. It is not a rule imposed on clients. It is a description of the practice that lets you stay present. The most useful limits are specific and visible: how many deep sessions you hold in a day, what time your last session ends, how you handle between-session contact, and how many clients you carry at once.

Maya set three. No sessions after 6pm. No more than four emotionally intensive sessions in a day. And a clear line in her welcome agreement: messages are answered on weekdays within one working day, and anything urgent belongs in the session or with an appropriate professional. She did not announce these as boundaries. She simply built them into how she worked. Two clients asked about the response time; both accepted it without friction.

  • Name your daily ceiling for deep sessions and protect it in your calendar.
  • Set a hard end time for the working day and let the last session finish before it.
  • Write your between-session contact policy into your agreement, not into a conversation you dread.

Build recovery into the week, not the weekend

Recovery that only happens on Saturday is not recovery. It is collapse. A sustainable coaching practice has small, repeated resets: ten minutes between sessions, a walk after the heaviest block, a day each week with no client work. These are not luxuries. They are the maintenance that keeps your attention available for the person in front of you.

Try a simple test. For one week, schedule a ten-minute gap after every session and use it for nothing productive. No notes, no email, no planning. Just a glass of water and a window. Notice whether your afternoon sessions feel different. Most coaches find that the gap does more for their presence than an extra hour of preparation ever did.

Review the instrument quarterly

Your energy is not a fixed reading. It changes with your life, your caseload, and the season. A quarterly review of your session notes takes twenty minutes and tells you more than a yearly resolution. Which clients leave you lighter? Which leave you heavier? What changed in the weeks when the heaviness rose? You are not looking for a verdict on anyone. You are looking for the shape of a practice that fits the person doing it.

If a pattern persists, the answer is usually a conversation, not a cancellation. A conversation about frequency, about what the client wants from the work, or about whether another kind of support would serve them better. You are allowed to shape your practice around your capacity. That is not a failure of care. It is the condition that makes care possible for the long run.
