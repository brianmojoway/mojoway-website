Corporate Coaching: The Two Numbers That Win Proposals

2026-10-16 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

You have built a steady one-to-one coaching practice. Clients refer you. Your calendar is full. Then someone from a company asks if you can work with their leadership team. It feels like the natural next step. But the conversation shifts quickly. The language changes. The questions are different. And the proposal you send may not land the way you expect.

Moving into organisational work is not about doing more of the same. It is about understanding what corporate buyers actually need to see before they commit budget. They are not looking for a longer version of your one-to-one offer. They are looking for a clear link between your work and two numbers they already track. Get those right, and you avoid the mistake that ends most first proposals.

What Corporate Buyers Actually Ask

When a coach moves from one-to-one work into an organisation, the first meeting often feels familiar. The buyer is warm, curious, and interested in your approach. But then come the questions that reveal a different set of priorities. They ask about scale, consistency, and how you will work with people who are not in the room. They ask what happens when a manager resists. They ask how you will measure progress without disrupting the team.

These questions are not about your coaching skill. They are about risk. A corporate buyer is responsible for spending money that belongs to the company. They need to know you can deliver something predictable, not just something meaningful. Your one-to-one clients hired you because they felt a personal connection. A corporate buyer hires you because they need a solution that works across a group, at a specific time, with a clear outcome.

For example, a coach I know was invited to propose a leadership programme for a mid-sized tech company. In the first meeting, the HR director asked: How will you handle the two managers who think they do not need coaching? That question was not about methodology. It was about whether the coach had a plan for resistance. The coach who answers that question well moves forward. The one who talks only about the power of reflection does not.

  • How will you work with people who are sceptical or resistant?
  • What does a typical session look like across a group?
  • How will you keep the work consistent when multiple coaches or locations are involved?
  • What happens if a participant drops out halfway through?

The Two Numbers They Care About

Corporate buyers are not indifferent to personal growth. But they have to justify the spend to someone else. In practice, two numbers come up again and again. The first is retention. The second is time to productivity. Everything else is secondary.

Retention matters because replacing a manager is expensive. A buyer will listen when you explain how your coaching supports people to stay, especially in roles where turnover is high or where a single departure disrupts a team. You do not need to promise a specific retention rate. You need to show that you understand why it matters and how your work connects to it. For example, a coach working with a healthcare organisation framed her programme around supporting new team leaders through their first year. The buyer cared less about the coaching model and more about whether those leaders would still be there in twelve months.

Time to productivity matters because every week a new leader takes to find their feet is a week of lost output. A corporate buyer wants to know how your coaching shortens that ramp-up period. This is where one-to-one experience becomes an advantage. You already know how to help someone think more clearly under pressure. Now you need to show how that translates into a group of new managers who need to make decisions faster and with more confidence. When you speak to these two numbers, you move from being a nice-to-have to a business investment.

The Mistake That Kills Most First Proposals

The mistake is not pricing too high or lacking credentials. It is sending a proposal that describes what you do instead of what the buyer gets. Most coaches write proposals that read like a biography of their practice. They list their training, their philosophy, and the structure of their sessions. The buyer reads it and thinks: This sounds lovely, but what will change for us?

A corporate buyer needs to see the outcome before they see the method. They need to know what success looks like six months from now, how it will be noticed, and what their people will be doing differently. If your proposal starts with your five-element framework or your approach to reflective practice, you have already lost them. Start with their problem. Then show how your work addresses it.

Consider the difference between these two openings. One coach wrote: I offer a six-session programme based on the MOJOWAY framework, helping leaders develop self-awareness and emotional intelligence. Another wrote: Your new managers are struggling to give feedback. By the end of this programme, they will be able to run a difficult conversation without avoiding it. The second one speaks the buyer's language. The first one speaks the coach's language. The buyer is not hiring you to be understood. They are hiring you to solve a problem they already know they have.

How to Reframe Your Offer for an Organisation

Reframing does not mean abandoning your approach. It means translating it. Your one-to-one work is built on trust, reflection, and personal insight. In an organisation, those same qualities are still valuable, but they need to be described in terms of team performance, communication, and decision-making. You are not changing what you do. You are changing the language around it.

Start by asking the buyer what they are already measuring. If they track retention, ask which teams have the highest turnover. If they track time to productivity, ask where new leaders take longest to settle. Then build your proposal around those specific points. Instead of offering a generic leadership programme, offer a targeted intervention for the group that needs it most. This shows that you listened, and it makes your proposal easier to approve.

One practical step is to write a one-page summary before you write the full proposal. On that page, state the problem in the buyer's words, the two numbers you will help them move, and the first three things you will do. If the buyer reads that page and nods, you have a strong foundation. If they look confused, you have more work to do. This simple habit prevents the most common mistake and keeps your proposal focused on the buyer's world, not yours.

  • Ask what they already measure before you describe your programme.
  • Use their language for the problem, not your language for the solution.
  • Tie your work to one or two business numbers, not to personal growth alone.

Building the Relationship Beyond the First Yes

Winning the first corporate contract is a milestone. But the coaches who build sustainable organisational work do something else. They treat the first programme as the beginning of a relationship, not the end of a sale. They check in after the programme ends. They ask what changed and what did not. They stay curious about the buyer's world.

This matters because corporate needs shift. A programme that worked for new managers may not be right for the next group. A buyer who trusted you with one team may trust you with a larger initiative later. But that trust is built on how you show up after the contract is signed. Do you deliver what you said you would? Do you communicate clearly? Do you make their life easier? Those things matter as much as your coaching skill.

For example, a coach who ran a six-week programme for a retail company sent a short summary at the end. It included what participants said, what changed, and one recommendation for the next quarter. The buyer forwarded it to their director. Six months later, that director asked the coach to work with a different division. The coach did not chase the next contract. They simply did good work and made it easy for the buyer to say yes again. That is how one-to-one coaches become long-term organisational partners.
