Reading the Room: Five Energies in Group Coaching

2026-09-28 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

You are twenty minutes into a workshop. The room is quiet in a way that is not calm. Two people are nodding too much. One person has not spoken since the opening round. You can feel something, but naming it directly feels risky. Say the wrong thing and you either embarrass someone or flatten the group.

This is where the five energies from the MOJOWAY framework can help. Not as a personality test, and not as a way to label people, but as a shared language for what is happening between people. The five elements, Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water, give you gentle words for group weather. You can name a dynamic without pointing at a person, and you can offer the group a way to move.

Why naming group dynamics is hard for facilitators

Most coaches are trained to notice. We track tone, pace, who leans in, who checks the door. But noticing is not the same as naming. In a one-to-one session you can reflect an observation back to one person. In a group, every observation lands on several people at once. Even a neutral comment like I notice some hesitation can be heard as a judgment by the person who hesitated.

The five elements give you a way around that. Instead of saying you seem withdrawn, you can say the room feels a bit Metal right now, careful and precise. That is a statement about the group, not about a person. It invites anyone to agree, disagree, or simply notice. It also gives the group a shared vocabulary they can use for the rest of the session.

A quick example. In a leadership workshop I ran in Manchester, the group went very still after a conflict exercise. Rather than asking who felt uncomfortable, I said the energy has gone quiet and contained, almost Metal. One participant laughed and said yes, we are all being very polite. That opened the conversation without anyone being singled out.

A quick map of the five energies in a group

You do not need to teach the whole framework to use it. A two-minute introduction is enough. The five energies are patterns of movement, not boxes. Every group has all five, and the balance shifts minute to minute.

Wood is the energy of direction and initiative. In a group it shows up as someone pushing for a decision, or a sense of forward momentum. Fire is connection and expression. It is laughter, warmth, eye contact, and also the risk of a room getting too hot and scattered. Earth is holding and containing. It is the person who checks whether everyone has had lunch, and the slow, steady pace that makes a group feel safe.

Metal is clarity and discernment. It shows up as precision, standards, and sometimes a cool, polite distance. Water is depth and reflection. It is the long pause, the thoughtful question, the feeling that something is moving under the surface. When you name these as group weather, people relax. They are not being diagnosed. They are being invited to notice.

  • Wood: direction, initiative, healthy push
  • Fire: warmth, expression, visible connection
  • Earth: steadiness, care, holding the group
  • Metal: clarity, standards, polite distance
  • Water: depth, quiet, reflective pause

How to name a dynamic without putting anyone on the spot

The key move is to speak about the field, not the person. Use phrases like the room feels, there is a lot of, or I notice the energy has gone. Then offer a choice. You can say the group seems very Metal right now, precise and careful. Would it help to bring in a little Fire, or is this exactly what we need? That gives the group agency. It also protects the quieter members, who often feel exposed when a facilitator names them directly.

Timing matters. Name the dynamic when it is useful, not every time you notice something. If the group is working well, let it work. If you name too often, you become the weather reporter and the group stops noticing for itself. Aim for one or two light observations in a ninety-minute session. Each one should open a door, not close one.

A worked example. In a team coaching session in Toronto, one participant kept bringing the conversation back to risks. The group was getting tense. Instead of saying you are being negative, I said there is a strong Metal voice here, and it is doing useful work. Then I asked what the Wood energy in the room wanted to do with that information. The risk-focused participant relaxed, and the group moved forward. Nobody was named. The dynamic was named.

Turning the observation into a choice for the group

Naming is only half the work. The other half is offering a next step. The five elements give you a simple menu. If the group is stuck in Metal, you might invite a little Fire with a pair share or a round of appreciations. If the group is scattered in Fire, you might invite Earth with a slow breathing pause or a grounding question. If the group is heavy in Water, you might invite Wood with a clear question about what they want to decide today.

You can also let the group choose. Ask which energy they want more of in the next twenty minutes. This works surprisingly well. People often know what they need. They just need permission and a shared language to say it.

One caution. Do not use the elements to avoid conflict. If two people disagree, naming the energy is not a substitute for addressing the disagreement. Use the framework to make the room safe enough to have the real conversation, then have it. The elements are a doorway, not a destination.

What to do when you get it wrong

You will sometimes misread the room. Maybe you call it Metal when it is actually Water, or you name a dynamic that lands badly. That is fine. In fact, it can be useful. Say something like I might be reading this wrong, what do you notice? That models humility and invites the group to correct you. The framework is a reflection tool, not a truth claim.

Keep your language tentative. Use seems, might, feels like. Avoid saying the group is a Fire group or that person is a Wood type. Those labels stick, and they do the opposite of what you want. The five energies are meant to loosen a group, not fix it in place.

Over time, you will build a small set of phrases that work for you. The room has gone quiet and careful. There is a lot of forward push here. Something deeper is wanting to be said. Those phrases become part of your facilitation voice, and the group starts using them too.
