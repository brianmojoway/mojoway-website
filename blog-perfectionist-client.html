Coaching the Perfectionist Without Joining the Perfectionism

2026-10-17 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

There is a moment in coaching a perfectionist when the work starts to feel very tidy. The client arrives with a beautifully prepared reflection, a color-coded plan, and a list of what they should have done better. You notice yourself sitting up straighter. The session is going well, you think. And that is exactly the moment to look at your own hands, because the tidiness may not be theirs alone.

Perfectionism coaching is not about fixing a client who tries too hard. It is about noticing the shared system that forms when two conscientious people meet, and one of them is holding the frame. Your standards are part of the room. The question is whether they are serving the client or quietly raising the bar the client now feels they must clear.

Notice the moment you start polishing

A life coach I know was working with a marketing director who wanted to stop rewriting every email three times. Halfway through the session, the coach caught herself suggesting a cleaner way to phrase the client's goal. It was a small edit, offered kindly. But the client's shoulders dropped, and she spent the next ten minutes refining the wording to please the coach. The perfectionism had changed seats.

Watch for your own polishing instinct: the urge to sharpen a client's language, correct a timeline, or improve a plan that was already workable. It often shows up as helpfulness. In the moment, ask a simple question: whose standard is this serving? If the answer is yours, you have found the edge of the work.

  • You rephrase the client's goal into something more elegant.
  • You add a step to their plan because it would be more complete.
  • You feel a flicker of discomfort when they leave something messy.
  • You notice the client watching your face for approval.

Separate high standards from self-worth

High standards are not the problem. A surgeon, a barrister, a parent managing a household all need them. The difficulty begins when a standard becomes a verdict on the person holding it. An executive coach in Toronto described a client who could not send a report without reading it aloud twice, not because the report needed it, but because anything less felt like evidence of carelessness.

In session, name the difference plainly. Ask what the standard is for, and what happens inside the client when they fall short. You are not trying to lower the bar. You are helping them see that the bar and their worth have been fused, and that the fusion, not the bar, is what exhausts them.

Use the Five Elements to see the pattern, not to label the person

The MOJOWAY framework offers five lenses, and perfectionism can wear any of them. A client may overwork through a Wood pattern of relentless forward motion, or freeze in Metal's demand for the correct form before beginning. Another may keep polishing through Earth's wish to be beyond reproach, or push through Fire's need to be seen as excellent. None of these is a diagnosis. They are reflections a client can hold up and turn over.

The point is not to type the client but to give them language for a pattern they can observe. When a client says, I think I am in the Metal version of this, they have stepped back from the behavior. That step back is the coaching. Your job is to keep the lens light, curious, and always in the client's hands.

Keep the session imperfect on purpose

One practical move is to leave something unfinished. A coach in London ended sessions by asking, What is one thing we are deliberately leaving messy this week? The client, a senior project manager, began to report small acts of acceptable incompleteness: a draft sent without a final read, a meeting agenda shared as a rough list. The coach did not praise the mess. She asked what the client noticed.

This is not a technique to install. It is an invitation to test a different relationship with the standard. Keep your own summaries plain. Resist the urge to close every loop. When you let a session end without a perfect action plan, you give the client permission to do the same.

Look at your practice, not just the client

Perfectionism in a client can flatter a coach's practice. The client is prepared, the notes are detailed, the goals are ambitious. But overworking clients often arrive exhausted, and a practice that rewards their exhaustion will keep producing it. Ask yourself where your own systems quietly ask for more than the work needs: the intake form that runs to six pages, the between-session check-in you expect to be answered promptly, the goal template with twelve fields.

A coaching practice is a small culture. You set its pace. If you want clients to tolerate good enough, they need to see you tolerate it too, in your scheduling, your follow-up, and the way you speak about your own work. That is not a promise of change. It is a steady example.

  • Review your intake and goal templates for fields that reward completeness over usefulness.
  • Notice whether you answer client emails at hours you would not ask them to.
  • Ask a peer coach to sit in on a session and watch for your polishing instinct.

Where is your practice asking for more than it needs?
