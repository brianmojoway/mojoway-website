Learning to shoot by not blinking

Liezi, chapter 5, "Tang's Questions" (汤问), 4th century BCE

Ji Chang asked the archer Fei Wei to teach him. Fei Wei said: before you study the art, you must learn not to blink. Keep your guard up and watch a shuttle on a loom, and then come back.

Ji Chang went home and lay under his wife's loom, eyes on the shuttle, which goes back and forth dozens of times a minute.

Two years. Then the shuttle stopped being fast to him. In fact, when he looked at a bird, it just hung in the air.

He went back. Fei Wei said: not yet. Now learn to see. Look at something small until it looks large, and look at something faint until it looks obvious. When you've done that, come back.

Three more years. A louse on a hair became the size of a wheel. Small things became mountains.

So two years to hold still, three to see, and only then the bow.

The story is usually quoted as proof that practice matters. The part that's actually instructive is what he practised first. Nobody handed him a weapon. He was asked to change his eyes.

Whatever the skill you've been stuck on, ask whether the missing piece is technique or capacity. Most learning plateaus are capacity: attention span, patience, unblinking on a boring subject for longer than feels reasonable.

MOJOWAY asks clients to practise looking before deciding what to change. That order matters. Awareness before action, capacity before method.
