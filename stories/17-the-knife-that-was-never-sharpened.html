The knife that was never sharpened

Zhuangzi, chapter 3, "Nourishing the Lord of Life" (养生主), 4th century BCE

Somebody does in half an hour what takes you a day, and it looks like nothing is happening.

You assume talent. Then you assume they're cutting corners. Then you go home and do it again the slow way, and the slow way leaves you with nothing left at nine in the evening.

A cook named Ding was butchering an ox for Lord Wenhui. His hands, his shoulders, his feet, his knees, all of it moving together, and the ox came apart with a sound like music.

The lord said, that's extraordinary, how does technique reach this point.

Ding put down the knife. What I care about, he said, is the pattern, and the pattern goes beyond technique. I don't see the whole ox. I see the joints. Where the bone and the tendon leave a gap, I put the blade through the gap. The edge has no thickness. The gap has room. So there's space left over, and I keep going.

This knife, he said, is nineteen years old. It has cut several thousand oxen. The edge is as good as the day it was ground.

The lord said he'd learned something about keeping a life intact.

What Ding isn't doing is forcing. He never chops through something that would open on its own. Nineteen years without sharpening is the whole proof.

Whatever you're stuck on today, look for the joint before you push again.
