The Ten-Minute Weekly Energy Audit for Coaching Clients

2026-10-18 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

Most clients come to coaching with a story about time. They are busy, stretched, and convinced that the answer is a better calendar. Then they spend three weeks optimizing their schedule and feel no different. The problem is not usually how the hours are arranged. It is what those hours cost them in energy, and almost nobody keeps a record of that.

The ten-minute weekly energy audit is a reflection tool you can teach in a single session. It does not require an app, a wearable, or a personality assessment. It asks a client to notice four things about their week, write them down, and bring the page to the next call. After three weeks, a pattern usually surfaces that neither of you could have predicted from conversation alone.

What the audit tracks, and what it deliberately ignores

The audit has four lines. First, the client lists two or three moments from the week when they felt most awake and engaged. Second, two or three moments when they felt flat, resentful, or drained. Third, one thing they did that they would have skipped if no one would notice. Fourth, one thing they wanted to do and did not. That is the whole instrument. It takes ten minutes on a Friday or Sunday, and it is done by hand.

What it ignores matters just as much. Clients should not track hours, productivity scores, or whether the week was good or bad. They should not try to rate their energy from one to ten, because the number becomes the project and the detail disappears. They should not audit every day, and they should not write paragraphs. A phrase per entry is enough. The audit is a noticing practice, not a performance review.

One client, a director at a mid-sized firm, spent her first week trying to log every meeting and rate it. The page was unusable by Friday. In our next session we cut it back to the four lines and she finished in eight minutes. The simpler version is the one that survives a hard week, which is exactly when the information is most useful.

How to teach it in one session

Teach the audit in the last fifteen minutes of a session, not the first. The client needs to have talked through their week first, so they have raw material in mind. Walk them through the four lines out loud and ask them to fill in one example for each line from the week they just described. This turns an abstract instruction into a demonstrated habit.

Then set the terms. The page comes to the next session, even if it is half-finished. A blank page is data too, and it usually means the week was either unusually chaotic or the client is avoiding something. Agree on a specific ten-minute slot, ideally the same one each week, and ask them to keep the page somewhere they will see it. Paper works better than a phone for most people, because the phone is where the week already lives.

Finally, tell them what you will do with it. You are not going to grade it. You are going to read it with them and look for repetition. That framing lowers the stakes and makes honesty more likely. Clients who think they are being assessed write what sounds reflective. Clients who think they are being accompanied write what is true.

  • Two or three moments of engagement, in a phrase each
  • Two or three moments of drain, in a phrase each
  • One thing done only because no one would notice if it was skipped
  • One thing wanted and not done

Reading the log together without turning it into a diagnosis

When the client brings the page back, resist the urge to interpret it for them. Ask what they notice first. Ask which entry they were most surprised to write. Ask what they would have expected to see that is not there. These questions keep the client in the role of the observer, which is where the useful information lives.

It helps to read the page in silence for a minute before speaking. Most coaches fill silence too quickly, and the client's own first comment is usually more accurate than anything we could offer. If they say 'I did not realize how much of my week was spent recovering from meetings,' let that stand. Do not upgrade it into a theory about their childhood or their attachment style.

Keep the language plain and provisional. 'It looks like' and 'I wonder if' are better than 'the pattern is.' The audit is a reflection tool, and its value comes from the client's own recognition. A coach who names the pattern too confidently takes the recognition away from the person who needs it.

The pattern that usually shows up after three weeks

By the third page, most clients see the same thing: the drains are not spread evenly across the week. They cluster. One client found that four of her five lowest-energy entries happened in the ninety minutes after her Monday leadership meeting. Another found that his best entries all involved work he had chosen rather than work assigned, regardless of how difficult it was. Neither of them could have told you that in week one.

The second common pattern is asymmetry between what clients do and what they value. The 'skipped if no one would notice' line and the 'wanted and did not do' line often point at the same thing from opposite directions. A client keeps doing a task that no one would miss while postponing the work that matters to her. Seeing both lines on the same page makes the trade visible in a way that conversation alone rarely does.

The third pattern is recovery. Clients who log for three weeks usually discover that their low-energy entries are followed by a specific kind of break, or the absence of one. Some recover through movement, some through solitude, some through conversation. The log does not tell them what to do about it. It shows them what already works, which is more convincing than advice.

What to do with the pattern in session four

Do not build a plan yet. The fourth session is for naming one pattern out loud and testing it against the client's experience. Ask: 'Does this match how the month felt?' If they hesitate, the pattern is probably yours rather than theirs, and it should be set aside. If they light up, you have found the thread for the next several sessions.

From there, the work becomes specific. One client decided to move a recurring one-to-one out of her Monday afternoon and into Wednesday morning, where her log showed she had more room. That is a small, testable change, and the next three weeks of logs will show whether it helped. The audit continues through the change, which is what makes it a practice rather than an exercise.

The point of the ten-minute audit is not to optimize a client's week. It is to give them a way of noticing that belongs to them, long after coaching ends. A page, a pen, ten minutes, and four lines. Most clients keep some version of it going for months, and the version they keep is usually simpler than the one you taught.
