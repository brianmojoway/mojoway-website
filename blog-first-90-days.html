Your First 90 Days: Find Your Coaching Niche with Five Energies

2026-09-29 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

In your first 90 days as a coach, the pressure to say yes to everyone is real. A friend refers a sales director. A former colleague asks for career clarity. Someone else wants help with work-life balance. You take them all, because saying no feels like turning down income and experience. But by week six you notice something: some sessions leave you energized, while others leave you drained and vaguely resentful. That pattern is information. It is the beginning of a niche, not a distraction from it.

MOJOWAY is a personal reflection framework rooted in the Daoist five elements, used by coaches to notice patterns in how people (including themselves) move through change. It is not a clinical, medical, or diagnostic tool, and it does not tell you who to work with. What it offers is a structured way to reflect on your own energy, strengths, and friction points during those first 90 days, so you can choose a niche that fits how you actually work, not just what the market seems to want.

Why chasing everyone in your first 90 days backfires

When you say yes to every client, you collect a random sample of human problems. That sounds like useful market research, but it usually produces something else: a scattered practice with no clear story. You cannot describe what you do in one sentence, so referrals dry up. You cannot build repeatable session structures, so every week feels like starting over. And you cannot tell whether you are good at this, because you are comparing apples to oranges.

Consider a new coach we will call Maya. In her first two months she took on a project manager who wanted promotion, a founder who wanted better delegation, and a nurse who wanted boundaries. By day 60 she was exhausted and could not explain her practice to anyone. When she finally looked at her notes, she saw that the sessions she loved were the ones about boundaries and self-advocacy. The promotion and delegation sessions were fine, but they felt like borrowed work. That is the signal the five elements help you read.

A quick, plain-English look at the five energies

You do not need to master Daoist philosophy to use this. Think of the five elements as five flavors of energy that show up in every coaching conversation. Wood is about direction, growth, and pushing forward. Fire is about visibility, connection, and spark. Earth is about stability, care, and holding things together. Metal is about clarity, standards, and letting go. Water is about depth, reflection, and moving with what is.

Most coaches naturally lean toward one or two of these. You might love the Fire of helping someone finally say what they want out loud. You might feel most useful in the Earth of steady, patient support. You might be a Metal coach who is happiest when a client cuts through confusion and makes a clean decision. None of these is better. But your natural lean shapes which clients you serve well and which ones quietly wear you down.

The reflection is simple. Over the next two weeks, after each session, write down which element felt most alive in you, and which felt most strained. Do not judge it. Just notice.

  • Wood: Do you enjoy clients who need momentum and a clear next step?
  • Fire: Do you light up when a client needs courage to be seen?
  • Earth: Do you feel at home providing steady, consistent support?
  • Metal: Do you enjoy cutting through noise and setting standards?
  • Water: Do you prefer deep, meandering conversations that take time?

Map your energy to a client problem, not a job title

New coaches often define a niche by job title: I coach tech founders, or I coach mid-career marketers. That is a start, but it is not the whole picture. Two tech founders can need completely different things. One needs Wood momentum. The other needs Water reflection. If you build your niche around a title, you will keep attracting clients whose real need is outside your natural energy.

A more useful move is to map your strongest element to a recurring client problem. If you are an Earth coach, your niche might be leaders who are burning out because they hold everyone else together. If you are a Metal coach, it might be professionals who are stuck in indecision and need clean criteria. If you are a Fire coach, it might be people who are ready to be visible but keep hiding.

Take Maya. Her strongest element was Fire, with a secondary in Wood. Her natural clients were not just anyone with a career question. They were people who had been quiet for too long and needed to claim a voice. Once she named that, her referrals became specific: a team lead who never spoke in meetings, a consultant who undercharged, a manager who avoided conflict. Her practice stopped feeling random.

A 90-day reflection plan you can actually keep

You do not need a complicated system. You need a lightweight habit that survives a busy week. Set aside ten minutes every Friday. Open your notes from the week and answer three questions: Which session gave me energy? Which session drained me? Which element was most present in each?

After four weeks, look for patterns. After eight weeks, write a one-sentence niche statement. After twelve weeks, test it with three people in your network. You are not locking yourself in forever. You are giving yourself a clear starting point, which is far more useful than a vague intention to help everyone.

One caution: this is a reflection tool, not a verdict. Your energy will shift with your life, your training, and your clients. The point is to notice, not to box yourself in.

  • Weeks 1-4: Track energy after every session. No changes yet.
  • Weeks 5-8: Identify your two strongest elements and the client problems they match.
  • Weeks 9-12: Write a one-sentence niche statement and share it with three trusted people.

What to do when a client does not fit your emerging niche

You will still get inquiries from people outside your niche, especially early on. You do not have to turn them away automatically. But you can be honest with yourself about the cost. If a client needs a lot of Wood momentum and you are an Earth coach, you can still help, but you will be working against your grain. That is fine occasionally. It is not fine as a business model.

A simple practice: before you say yes, ask which element this client most needs, and whether you can offer it without depleting yourself. If the answer is no, refer them to a coach who fits better. That referral is not a lost sale. It is a reputation built on honesty, and it often comes back to you as a better-matched client later.

Over time, your niche becomes less about what you exclude and more about what you are known for. People do not refer you because you coach everyone. They refer you because they know exactly who you help and why.
