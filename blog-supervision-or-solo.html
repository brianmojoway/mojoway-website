Supervision, Peer Group, or Nothing: What Solo Coaches Lose

2026-10-19 · 6 min read · by Brian Zhang

When you work alone, the person least likely to notice your blind spots is you. Not because you are careless, but because the same mind that designs your questions also decides which questions worked. Coaching supervision, peer supervision, and a steady reflective practice are the three structures coaches use to bring another pair of eyes to that problem. The question is which one you need, and what quietly erodes when you choose none of them.

This is not a pitch for more meetings. Some coaches thrive with a monthly supervision hour, some need a peer group, and some need a written reflective practice that costs nothing and fits between sessions. What matters is that you know what each structure offers, what it does not, and what you are willing to let go of when you stop having anyone look at your work with you.

What actually gets lost when no one sees your work

The first loss is calibration. A coach I know spent eighteen months working with a founder who answered every question with a polished summary. The coach believed the sessions were going well. In a peer supervision session, she described one exchange out loud and a colleague asked, 'When did he last say something he had not already said to his board?' She had not noticed that her client had not had an unscripted thought in months. Nothing was wrong with her questions. She had simply lost the outside view that reveals a pattern you are inside of.

The second loss is drift. Without another person asking why you chose a particular approach, small habits harden into a style. You start every session with the same opening question, avoid a certain kind of emotion because it makes you uncomfortable, or keep a client well past the point where the work is useful. None of these are failures of skill. They are the natural result of working without a mirror.

The third loss is the quiet accumulation of unprocessed material. Coaches hold a lot of other people's uncertainty. Without a place to put it, it tends to leak into the next session, the next client, or your own evenings. Reflective practice is the minimum response to that, and it is the one most solo practitioners skip because it feels like homework.

Coaching supervision: the structured outside view

Coaching supervision is a formal relationship with a more experienced practitioner whose job is to help you see your work more clearly. It is not therapy and it is not a performance review. A good supervisor asks you to bring a real session, describe what you did, and examine the choices underneath it. The focus is your practice, not your client's diagnosis.

The value is in the structure. You book it, you prepare for it, and you cannot quietly avoid the client who unsettles you because the hour is already on the calendar. For coaches handling complex organisational dynamics or long-term executive relationships, that regular outside view is often the difference between a considered practice and an accumulated set of habits.

It is also the most expensive option, in both money and time. That is not a reason to avoid it, but it is a reason to be honest about whether you will use it well. One coach I spoke to paid for supervision for a year and brought the same comfortable client every month. The structure was there. The honesty was not.

  • Best for coaches in complex, long-term, or high-stakes engagements
  • Requires a supervisor you are willing to be genuinely uncertain in front of
  • Works when you bring real sessions, not polished summaries of them

Peer supervision: the honest mirror of equals

Peer supervision is a small group of coaches, usually three to five, who meet regularly to look at each other's work. There is no senior figure, which is both the strength and the risk. The strength is that peers tend to challenge you more directly, because there is no hierarchy to protect. The risk is that a group without structure becomes a friendly catch-up where everyone leaves feeling supported and no one leaves feeling seen.

A simple format helps. Each meeting, one person brings a session or a stuck moment. They describe it for ten minutes without interruption. The others ask questions for fifteen minutes, then offer observations for ten, and the person who brought the work says what they are taking away. Rotate who brings work. Keep the time. That is enough structure to make peer supervision genuinely useful.

The other thing peer supervision gives you is a sense of proportion. When you spend all week inside your own head, a difficult client can feel like a personal failing. Hearing three other coaches describe similar moments does something that no book does. It normalises the work without excusing the pattern.

Reflective practice: the minimum that still counts

Reflective practice is what you do on your own, and it is the option most coaches underestimate. It can be a short written note after each session: what I noticed, what I chose, what I would do differently. It can be a weekly review of one client. It can be a structured self-supervision prompt, like the five-element reflection in MOJOWAY, which asks you to look at a situation through five different lenses rather than your default one.

The point is not to produce a journal. The point is to interrupt the automatic. A coach who writes three honest lines after a session is already doing something that a coach who simply moves to the next calendar entry is not. Over a year, that difference compounds.

What reflective practice cannot do is surprise you. You are still the one asking the questions, which means your blind spots remain blind. It is the floor, not the ceiling. Used well, it also makes supervision and peer groups sharper, because you arrive with something specific to examine rather than a vague sense that things are fine.

  • Three lines after a session is enough to start
  • Use a fixed set of prompts so you cannot avoid the uncomfortable lens
  • Bring your notes to supervision or peer group to make those hours count

How to choose without overcommitting

The honest answer is that most solo coaches need one structured outside view and one regular reflective habit. If you are early in practice, a peer group is often the best first step: it is affordable, it builds a professional community, and it gets you used to describing your work out loud. If you are working with senior leaders or complex systems, coaching supervision is worth the cost, provided you are willing to bring the sessions that actually unsettle you.

If neither is possible right now, do not choose nothing. Choose reflective practice and be honest with yourself about its limits. Write the three lines. Use a structured reflection once a week. Then revisit the question in six months, because the coach who has been reflecting alone for a year usually knows exactly what kind of outside view they are missing.

The goal is not to accumulate professional development hours. It is to make sure that someone, somewhere, is looking at your work with you. That might be a supervisor, a peer group, or a page in a notebook. What it should not be is nobody.
