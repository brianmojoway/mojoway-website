Coaching Scope Creep: Name It Early, Keep Trust

2026-10-19 · 6 min read · by Brian Zhang

You finish a session at 10:55, and by 11:20 you are still on the call because your client had one more thing. The next day a voice note arrives in the group chat asking for a quick read on a decision. By Friday you have answered three emails, reviewed a slide deck, and agreed to a short check-in that was not in your coaching agreement. None of it is dramatic. Each request is small and reasonable on its own. Together they quietly reshape the coaching scope you thought you had.

This is scope creep, and it rarely comes from a difficult client. More often it comes from a warm, engaged person who trusts you and wants more of your thinking. The risk is not the extra twenty minutes. The risk is that the coaching relationship starts to feel like an open-ended support line, and the work you are actually paid to do gets squeezed. Naming it early is the kind thing to do. Done well, it protects the relationship rather than making it transactional.

What scope creep looks like in a coaching relationship

Scope creep in coaching is any gradual expansion of what you provide beyond what you and the client agreed. It usually shows up as extra calls, longer sessions, free advice in the group chat, or informal work between sessions. It is rarely requested outright. It arrives as a favour, a quick question, or a one-off exception that becomes a pattern.

Consider a typical example. A leadership coach agrees to six fortnightly sessions with a client. By session three, the client asks to move a session because of a board meeting, then asks for a fifteen-minute call to prepare. The coach says yes. Two weeks later the client sends a message in the group chat asking for feedback on a memo before it goes out. The coach replies because it is quick. By session five, the client expects a reply within the day and has stopped preparing for sessions because the coach will fill the gaps. The coaching agreement said six sessions. The lived agreement is now something else.

Why it happens, and why it is not a character flaw

Clients expand scope for understandable reasons. They are anxious about a decision and want reassurance. They value your judgement and want it available. They may not know where the boundary sits because you have never said it out loud. In many cases they are simply treating you the way they treat a trusted colleague, and they assume you will say no if you need to.

Coaches contribute too. We care about the work, we want to be helpful, and saying yes feels easier in the moment than a careful conversation. Many of us also work without a written coaching agreement that spells out what is included, what is not, and how additional support is handled. When the agreement is vague, the client is not the only one guessing. The result is a relationship that drifts rather than one that is deliberately designed.

Name it early, and name it warmly

The earlier you name scope creep, the smaller the conversation. A pattern caught in week two is a light adjustment. The same pattern caught in month six can feel like a withdrawal of support. The aim is not to police every message. It is to make the shape of the coaching scope visible before it becomes a source of quiet resentment.

Warmth matters more than precision here. You are not reading out a contract. You are telling the client what you have noticed and what you want to protect. Try something like: I have noticed we have added a few calls between sessions, and I want to make sure we are using our time well. Can we agree on what sits inside our coaching scope and what we handle differently? That is not a rebuke. It is an invitation to design the relationship together.

A useful frame is to separate three things: what is included, what is possible at an additional cost, and what sits outside coaching entirely. Most clients respond well to this because it removes ambiguity. They are not trying to take advantage. They are trying to work out where the edges are.

  • Included: scheduled sessions, agreed between-session reflection, and a clear response window for messages.
  • Possible by arrangement: additional calls, document review, or extended sessions, with a simple process and fee.
  • Outside coaching: urgent decisions, therapy, legal or financial advice, and daily availability.

Keep it relational, not transactional

The fear many coaches have is that naming scope will make the relationship feel like a transaction. In practice, the opposite is often true. Clear agreements reduce the small resentments that make a relationship feel transactional in the first place. When you know what you are giving and the client knows what they are receiving, generosity becomes possible again because it is chosen rather than assumed.

Keep the conversation anchored in the client's goals. Say what you have noticed, say why it matters for their progress, and ask what they want. For example: I want to protect the depth of our sessions, and I have noticed that when I answer questions in the group chat, we spend less time on the real work. How would you like to handle between-session questions? That keeps the focus on the coaching, not on the invoice.

It also helps to revisit the coaching agreement once, briefly, at the midpoint of an engagement. A short review is normal practice, not a sign of trouble. It gives both of you a natural moment to adjust the coaching scope without anyone feeling accused.

A simple script for the first conversation

You do not need a long speech. A short, direct opening is usually enough. Try: I want to check in on how we are working together. I have noticed a few extra requests between sessions, and I want to make sure our coaching scope still fits what you need. Can we take five minutes to agree on it?

Then listen. The client may say they did not realise, or they may ask for more support. Both are useful. If they want more, you can offer it as a defined arrangement rather than an open-ended expectation. If they did not realise, you have saved yourself months of quiet frustration. Either way, the relationship is stronger because the boundary was named before it became a problem.

Finally, write down what you agree. A short follow-up message that restates the coaching scope is not bureaucracy. It is a kindness to both of you, and it makes the next conversation much easier.

What good looks like six months in

A healthy coaching relationship with clear boundaries is not distant. It is reliable. The client knows when they will hear from you, what is included, and how to ask for more. You know what you have committed to, and you can offer extra support generously when you choose to, rather than resentfully because you feel you have to.

Scope creep is not a sign that something has gone wrong. It is a normal pressure in any relationship where someone values your thinking. The skill is in naming it early, warmly, and in a way that keeps the focus on the client's work. That is what makes coaching boundaries sustainable, and it is what allows the coaching agreement to do its job: holding the relationship steady while the real work happens.
