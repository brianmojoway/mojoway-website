When the Client Only Talks About Other People

2026-11-19 · 8 min read · by Brian Zhang

Some sessions feel busy and go nowhere. The client arrives with a full account of what their manager said, how their partner reacted, and what a friend did last weekend. Twenty minutes pass and you have heard a great deal about other people and almost nothing about what the client wants. It is easy to read this as resistance and to push harder for feelings. That usually makes the stories longer.

Third-person talk is rarely an avoidance of the work. It is often the only safe way the client has found to approach the work. Other people are easier to describe than ourselves, and a story about a colleague carries less risk than a sentence that starts with I want. The task is not to stop the stories. It is to use them as a door.

What the third-person story is doing

Before you redirect, notice what function the story serves. A client who describes a difficult manager may be testing whether you will take sides. One who recounts a friend's decision may be asking, without asking, whether that choice was acceptable. The content is about other people; the need underneath is usually about the client's own judgment. Treat the story as indirect material rather than filler.

This distinction matters because it changes your response. If you treat the story as reportage, you will ask for more detail about the manager. If you treat it as indirect material, you will soon ask where the client stands. The second response is more useful and, done gently, feels like being understood rather than corrected.

Move from they to I in one small step

The simplest intervention is a pronoun shift with no change of topic. When the client says, She never listens in meetings, wait a beat and ask, And what do you do when that happens? You have not judged the story or demanded a feeling. You have added one missing person to the scene, and that person is the one who can act.

Keep the question narrow. Broad prompts such as How does that make you feel invite a summary and often a return to narrative. A narrow prompt about a specific moment, a specific choice, or a specific word they used gives the client something concrete to answer. The goal is one first-person sentence, not a breakthrough.

Use the five energies to choose the door

Different clients need different doors, and the five energies are a practical way to choose one. A client who leads with Fire energy often responds to a question about what they want to say next time, because agency is the live thread. One who leads with Water energy may need the relational question first: what did you hope the other person would understand?

Earth energy tends to open through what was practical and what did not work. Air energy opens through what they noticed and how they made sense of it. Wood energy opens through what the situation was moving toward and where they want to go. You are not labelling a type in the room; you are picking which question is most likely to land with this person today.

Name the pattern without shaming it

After a few sessions you may see the same shape: every story ends with someone else's behaviour and no line about the client's own next move. It is appropriate to name this, but the wording matters. Instead of You always talk about other people, try I notice we have mapped other people very carefully today and I do not yet know what you would change.

That sentence does two things. It shows you were listening, and it marks the absence without accusation. Most clients will fill the gap themselves. If they cannot, that is useful information too: the gap may be exactly what the coaching is for, and it can become the theme of the next session rather than a failure of this one.

Close with a line the client can test

End where you began, with one small first-person sentence. Not a plan for the month and not a list of insights. Something the client can carry into a real conversation in the next seven days: I will say the agenda item first, or I will ask for the deadline before agreeing. Write it in their words and read it back.

Then set the next session to start from that sentence rather than from a new story. When the third-person talk returns, as it will, you have a shared reference point to return to. Over time the client learns that the useful part of the conversation is the line about themselves, and the stories become material instead of camouflage.
