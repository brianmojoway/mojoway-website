The First-Session Contract: Setting the Frame in Five Energies

2026-11-20 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

The first coaching session is full of good intentions. The coach wants to demonstrate value; the client wants to seem coachable. Both rush past the one conversation that makes the rest possible: how this relationship will actually work. A clear frame is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between an hour of pleasant conversation and an hour the client can use.

In MOJOWAY's five energies model, Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water each meet a contract differently. What feels like a useful structure to one client can feel like a cage to another. This article gives you a practical contracting script and five ways to adapt it, so the frame fits the person rather than the template.

What the Contract Is For

A coaching contract answers four plain questions: what this hour is for, what stays private, what the client chooses, and what happens between sessions. Say them out loud in the first ten minutes and you remove most of the confusion that surfaces later as resistance or silence. Most clients have never been asked to define the purpose of a conversation.

Keep the language short and concrete. Avoid promising outcomes you cannot control, and avoid vague phrases such as we will explore whatever comes up. A frame that is too loose leaves the client carrying the whole burden of direction; a frame that is too tight leaves no room for what actually matters.

Wood: Contract for Scope and Momentum

A Wood-dominant client arrives ready to move. They want a plan, a target, and evidence that the hour will produce something. Give them a contract with a clear beginning and end: what we will work on, what a useful session looks like, and how we will know we got there.

Wood also needs to know where the boundaries of your role sit. Be direct about what you will and will not do. Saying that you will help design the next step but will not make the decision for them lands well. Wood respects a capable limit far more than an open-ended promise.

Fire: Contract for the Relational Container

For Fire, the contract is a relationship agreement before it is a work agreement. They need to know that the room is theirs, that what they say will be held with care, and that you will be fully present rather than mechanically following a process.

Name privacy plainly and warmly. Then add permission: the client can change their mind about what we discuss, and they can tell you when something you said missed. Fire clients test whether honesty is safe. A contract that invites correction tells them it is.

Earth: Contract for Permission to Be the Client

The Earth client will spend the first session making you comfortable. They will ask about your method, offer to adjust to your style, and quietly leave their own agenda last. The contract is your chance to put their needs at the center on purpose.

Say it explicitly: for this hour, the focus is theirs. Then set a small structure that protects it, such as agreeing to start each session with what is most alive for them rather than the most urgent task on their list. Earth rarely takes that focus without permission. Give it.

Metal: Contract for Precision and Choice

Metal clients read a contract as a set of terms, and they read sloppiness as a warning. Define the logistics exactly: length, frequency, what happens if a session is missed, what is private, and what is shared with anyone else. Precision here builds trust.

Just as important, make every clause a choice rather than a rule. Telling the client they can decline any question, and that they decide what we revisit next time, gives Metal the control they need in order to relax it. A contract they agreed to is a contract they will use.

Water: Contract for Depth and Pace

Water clients go below the surface, but not on command. A contract that asks for immediate disclosure will make them retreat. Frame the hour as a space where depth is welcome and silence is allowed, with no obligation to perform insight.

Agree on pace together. Letting the client know we can stay with one thing for the whole session if that is what the work needs gives Water permission to go deep instead of skimming. Then keep your word: do not fill every pause, and do not rush them toward a conclusion at the clock.

A Six-Line Script and a Habit

You do not need a legal document. Six sentences, spoken in the first ten minutes, cover the frame: what this hour is for; what stays private; that you will ask direct questions and they can decline any of them; that they set the agenda; what happens between sessions; and that the arrangement can be revised at any time.

The habit matters more than the wording. Revisit one line of the contract at the start of each session, especially when the work gets sensitive. A frame that is remembered only in session one is decoration. A frame you return to becomes the structure clients lean on when the conversation gets hard.

  • Ask what the client wants this hour to be for, in their own words.
  • State privacy in one plain sentence, then stop talking.
  • Give explicit permission to decline any question.
  • Put the client's agenda ahead of the task list, especially for Earth.
  • Agree on pace and silence before you ask for depth with Water.
  • Revisit one line of the frame whenever the work turns sensitive.
