When a Client Wants an Answer, Not Another Question

2026-11-18 · 9 min read · by Brian Zhang

Early in a coaching engagement, most clients learn the rhythm: the coach asks, the client thinks, and the answer arrives from somewhere inside the room. It works often enough that coaches start to treat it as a rule. Then a client stops, looks at you with some irritation, and says: I did not pay you to ask me another question. Just tell me what you would do. The instinct in that moment is to protect the method and ask a better question. That instinct usually makes things worse.

The request for an answer is not resistance. It is information. It tells you something about the client's energy in that moment, about how much uncertainty they can hold, and about what they think coaching is for. Handled well, it becomes one of the more useful moments in the work. Handled badly, it turns the session into a small power struggle that the client quietly loses. Here is how to work with the request instead of against it.

Why the question reflex fails here

Coaching training rewards the open question. It is a good default because it returns authorship to the client and keeps the coach from installing their own life as a template. But a default is not a law. When a client is tired, under time pressure, or facing a decision they have already thought about for days, another question can land as a refusal to help. They experience it as the coach keeping the one thing they came for just out of reach. At that point the question is no longer opening anything; it is a small wall.

There is also a cost to the relationship. A client who asks directly and gets a question back learns that direct requests do not work here. They either push harder until the coach gives in, which becomes a win-lose exchange, or they stop asking and comply with the process. Neither outcome is good. The session stays polite while the real question goes unasked. Noticing that shift early is more useful than defending the technique after the client has already withdrawn.

Ask what kind of answer they are requesting

Before you decide whether to give an answer, find out which answer they are asking for. Some clients want information: what other people in this situation have done, and what the options are. Some want a decision made for them because the cost of choosing feels higher than the cost of following. Some want permission: they already know what they want and need someone to say it is acceptable. These three requests sound identical in the room and need completely different responses.

A short question does this without stonewalling. When they say tell me what you would do, ask whether they are requesting options, your recommendation, or simple agreement with what they already think. Clients are often surprised by the question because nobody has separated those things for them before. Their answer shows how much of the work is genuinely yours to do and how much is still theirs. You have refused them nothing; you have made the request specific enough to be met.

Read the energy behind the request

The five energies give a quick read on why the request arrived now. A client leaning on Fire often wants a decision because momentum matters more to them than a perfect choice; they would rather move and adjust. A Water client may be asking because the feeling underneath the decision is still unnamed and they hope you can name it for them. A Wood client can be frustrated by questions because they have already done the thinking and want the next concrete step.

Earth and Metal show up differently. An Earth client may ask for an answer because they do not want to burden another person with their uncertainty, so the request is a form of consideration rather than impatience. A Metal client may want the principle first, the rule that would make the decision obvious, and will find a list of options unsatisfying. None of this means you should hand out a type label. It means the same sentence, I need your answer, carries a different request depending on who is saying it, and your response should match the person rather than the template.

Give a small answer, then hand it back

You can answer directly without taking over the session. The move is to keep the answer small, specific, and clearly bounded to your own view. Say what you would consider, in one or two sentences, and say what you cannot know from the outside. Then give it back: given that, which part still does not sit right. This respects the request, because you did answer, and it keeps authorship with the client, because your answer was offered as material rather than a verdict.

Be careful with confidence. A calm, certain tone from a coach is easy to mistake for evidence, and clients will follow a wrong answer delivered well. Mark the edges of what you are saying: I have not seen the other person, I only know your account, so treat this as one reading. That small hedge costs you nothing in authority and protects the client from adopting a decision that was never really based on their situation. It also models the difference between holding a view and being ruled by one.

When they insist on the full answer

Occasionally a client will not accept a bounded answer. They want you to say exactly what to do. At that point it is worth naming what is happening in the room, gently and without accusation. You might say: you want me to decide, and part of you is relieved to hand it over. What would it cost you if I did. That question is not a dodge, because you have already given your view; it opens the reason the client cannot carry the decision themselves, which is usually the real work of the session.

If the client still wants a decision after that conversation, you can give one, with the conditions attached. Say what you would do with the information you have, and agree on what they will watch for that would tell you both it was the wrong call. This keeps the door open for revision and avoids the sense that the choice was final. Some clients need one clear answer early in the work before they trust the questions again. Giving it well is not a failure of method; refusing to give it every time can be.
