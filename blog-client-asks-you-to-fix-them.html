When a Client Asks You to Fix Them

2026-11-11 · 7 min read · by Brian Zhang

The first time a client says, in some form, can you just tell me what to do, or can you fix this for me, it can feel like a compliment. They trust you. They believe you hold the answer. But the request is also a trap, because the moment you accept the job of fixing them, you take the one thing coaching depends on, their own authorship of the change, out of their hands.

This is one of the most common and most mishandled moments in early coaching, especially in the first two or three sessions before the rhythm of the work is set. Below is a practical way to respond that honours the trust, keeps the work where it belongs, and quietly protects both of you from a dynamic that goes stale within a month.

Why the request shows up

People arrive at coaching having been helped in other ways most of their lives: a parent who solved the problem, a manager who gave the instruction, a teacher who offered the interpretation. Asking an expert to take the problem is a learned habit, and for many clients it is the only shape of help they know. They are not being lazy. They are reaching for the model of help that has worked before, and handing you the problem is what that model looks like.

It also appears when the stakes feel high and the client is tired. A request to be fixed is sometimes a request to be relieved of the weight of deciding. Naming that gently, rather than complying with it, is often the first genuinely useful thing that happens in the engagement. The relief they are asking for is real. The method they propose is not the one that delivers it, and the gap between the two is where your response begins.

Name the shift without refusing

You do not have to argue with the request, and you should not pretend to accept it. A simple sentence does the work: I can work with you on this, and the way I work is that you stay in the driver seat, my job is to help you see the choices, not make them for you. Said plainly and without apology, this rarely offends. It tells the client what kind of help is on offer, and it does so before any expectation gets built.

The tone matters more than the words. If you sound defensive, the client hears rejection. If you sound bored, they hear that their need is small. Aim for matter-of-fact warmth: this is simply how the work is built, the same way a navigator does not steer the boat. Stating it early, in session one or two, saves you from a year of quietly rescuing someone who never asked you to stop, and who would not thank you for it if they noticed.

Move from fix to choice

Once the frame is set, the move is mechanical and repeatable. When a client hands you a problem to solve, hand it back as a choice. Instead of answering what should I do, ask what they would advise a friend in the exact same spot, or which of two paths they are already leaning toward. The question returns the authority to the person who has to live with the outcome, and it does so without a lecture about autonomy.

This is not a trick to avoid helping. It is the help. A client who leaves with a decision they made is in a different position from one who leaves with an instruction they were given. The first can act without you present. The second waits for the next session to be told again. Over a dozen sessions, that difference is the entire value of coaching, and it starts with refusing the fix in favour of the choice, every single time it is offered.

What to say when they push back

Some clients push back directly: but you are the expert, you must know. Here the honest answer is the strong one: I know the territory, and I do not know your life. My knowing the map does not tell me which turn is yours. That sentence, said kindly, often lands as a relief, because it removes the fantasy that anyone could simply decide for them and ends the search for a shortcut that does not exist.

Others go quiet or disappointed, and the silence can be tempting to fill by caving in. Resist it. A few seconds of discomfort is a small price for a year of real work. If the silence lasts, name it: it looks like you were hoping for a clearer answer than I can give, is that right. Naming the disappointment keeps it between you instead of letting it harden into a quiet resentment about a service that was never going to do what they imagined.

A short script you can keep

For the moments that matter, a compact version is enough. When they ask you to decide: I will not make the call, because you will have to live with it, but I will help you see it clearly. When they want certainty: I cannot hand you the answer, and I can help you choose. When they doubt themselves: the fact that you are unsure does not mean you are wrong, it means the choice matters.

None of these fix the client. All of them keep the work with the client, where the change actually happens. The request to be fixed will come back, probably many times. Each time it does, the same calm refusal is an act of respect, not withholding. You are not refusing to help. You are refusing to carry the part of the work that was never yours to carry, and leaving it with the person best placed to do it.
