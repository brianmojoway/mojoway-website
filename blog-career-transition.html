Career Transitions as Energy Events: A Five Elements Lens

2026-11-05 · 7 min read · by Brian Zhang

A career transition is rarely just a change of title or employer. It is an energy event. Something that was steady becomes unsettled, and the person in front of you is not only updating a CV, they are rearranging how they meet the world. Coaches see this constantly: the client who leaves a role with relief and then feels oddly flat, or the one who enters a new job with all the right credentials and a quiet sense that they are wearing someone else's coat.

MOJOWAY is a personal reflection framework built on the Five Elements, used by coaches as a lens rather than a label. It does not tell you who a client is or what they should do. It offers a vocabulary for the texture of a transition: how someone leaves, what they fear, and how they enter the next thing. In this article we look at career transition coaching through that lens, with a worked example for each element and a practical way to hold the in-between.

Why a career move is an energy event, not just a decision

Most career conversations start with the decision: should I stay, go, retrain, step back, step up? Useful, but incomplete. Underneath the decision is a shift in energy. A person who has spent years being the reliable one may find that reliability now feels like a cage. Another may be leaving a role they loved because the pace has changed and their body has started to notice. The decision is the visible part; the energy is what makes the decision feel urgent, heavy, or strangely numb.

This matters for coaches because the same practical move, say leaving a job, can look completely different depending on the energy involved. One client leaves with clarity and momentum. Another leaves with relief that turns into guilt. A third leaves quietly and then cannot explain to anyone, including themselves, why they did it. If we coach only the plan, we miss the part that determines whether the plan holds.

The Five Elements lens gives us five ways to notice this. None of them is better than another. They are simply different textures of leaving, fearing, and entering. Used lightly, they help a client say, that is what this feels like, which is often the first honest sentence of a transition.

Wood and Fire: leaving with direction, entering with visibility

Wood energy in a transition tends to look like growth that has become urgent. The client is done waiting. They want to move, build, stretch. The leaving is often decisive, sometimes a little abrupt, and the fear underneath is not failure but constraint: the dread of staying small. In coaching, Wood clients benefit from naming the direction before the plan. Ask what they are growing toward, not just what they are leaving behind.

Worked example: a midlife career client in operations tells you she has handed in her notice with no next role lined up. She is energised and slightly shocked at herself. Rather than steadying her too quickly, you ask what she is making room for. She says she wants to work with her hands again, something she last did in her twenties. The transition is not reckless; it is a growth impulse looking for a shape.

Fire energy is about visibility and connection. Leaving here often involves a loss of being seen, and entering involves the risk of being seen again in a new way. The fear is exposure: what if I am not received? A Fire client may delay the announcement, or over-prepare for interviews as a way of managing how they will be perceived. Coaches can help by separating the warmth of connection from the pressure to perform.

Worked example: a senior coach moving from a large firm to independent practice keeps postponing her launch. The plan is ready. What is not ready is the moment of being visible as herself rather than as a representative of a brand. Naming that as a Fire transition, not a discipline problem, changes the conversation from, why are you procrastinating, to, what would make being seen feel safe enough to begin.

Earth and Metal: the ground of security and the grief of leaving well

Earth energy shows up as the need for ground. Leaving a stable role can feel like the floor moving, even when the role was unsatisfying. The fear is not usually about ambition but about belonging and security: who am I without this team, this title, this routine? Earth transitions ask for steadiness, not speed. Coaching here often means helping a client build a small, reliable base before they make the next leap.

Worked example: a midlife career client in Canada is considering leaving a public-sector job he has held for fourteen years. He is not excited; he is unsettled. He keeps asking whether he is being ungrateful. You do not push him toward a decision. You ask what would need to be true for him to feel grounded enough to explore. He names three things: a financial buffer, a weekly conversation with a former colleague, and permission to not know yet. That is the transition plan for now.

Metal energy is about standards, boundaries, and letting go. Leaving here often involves grief, because something was done well and is now ending. The fear is loss of quality or meaning: will the next thing be as good? Metal clients may be quietly critical of themselves for not handling the transition more cleanly. Coaches can make room for the grief without turning it into a problem to solve.

Worked example: an executive coach in the UK is winding down a practice she built over a decade. She describes the ending as administrative, but in session she keeps returning to a particular client she will no longer see. You name the loss directly. She cries, briefly, and then says she had not given herself permission to count it as a loss. The transition is not only a business decision; it is a rite of passage.

Water: the unknown, the pause, and the wisdom of not knowing yet

Water energy is the least comfortable for many coaches because it does not produce a plan quickly. It is the energy of the unknown, of depth, of waiting for clarity rather than forcing it. A Water transition may look like a client who has left a role and cannot yet say what is next. They are not stuck; they are in the in-between. The fear is dissolution: if I stop being the person with the title, who am I?

Worked example: a midlife career client in Australia leaves a leadership role and spends three months saying very little about the future. Friends are worried. In coaching, you resist the urge to fill the silence with goals. You ask what is becoming clearer, not what is the plan. By the fourth session she describes a theme, not a job: she wants to work at a slower pace with fewer people and more depth. That is enough to begin.

The practical skill here is tolerating the pause without abandoning the client to it. Water transitions benefit from gentle structure: a regular session, a few reflective questions, a small experiment rather than a full strategy. The coach's job is not to hurry the tide.

  • Ask what is becoming clearer, rather than what is the plan.
  • Offer one small experiment instead of a full strategy.
  • Keep the rhythm of sessions steady so the pause has a container.
  • Name the fear of dissolution directly, without dramatising it.

Coaching the in-between with the right lens

The in-between is where most career transitions are actually won or lost, and it is where coaches add the most value. A useful practice is to ask three questions at the start of any transition conversation: how are you leaving, what are you afraid of, and how do you want to enter? Then listen for the element that seems most alive. You are not diagnosing; you are noticing a texture and offering it back.

A second practice is to avoid matching your own energy to the client's by default. If you are a Wood coach and your client is in a Water transition, your instinct to move may be unhelpful. If you are Earth and your client is Fire, your steadiness may feel like dampening. The lens helps you adjust: more ground for the Water client, more visibility for the Earth client, more room for grief with the Metal client.

Finally, remember what this framework is and is not. MOJOWAY is a personal reflection tool. It does not assess, diagnose, or promise an outcome. It gives coaches and clients a shared language for a season of change. Used with care, that language can make the in-between feel less like a void and more like a passage with its own weather.

  • Open with three questions: leaving, fear, entering.
  • Listen for the element that is most alive, then offer it back as a texture, not a label.
  • Adjust your own default energy rather than repeating it.
  • Keep the framework light: a lens for reflection, not a verdict.
