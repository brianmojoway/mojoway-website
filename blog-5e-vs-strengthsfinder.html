Five Energies vs CliftonStrengths: Two Very Different Questions

2026-11-10 · 7 min read · by Brian Zhang

A client arrives with their top five strengths printed on a card. They can recite them in order. They have been told to lean into them, build a role around them, stop apologising for them. And they are sitting in front of you because something still is not working. That gap, between knowing what you are good at and knowing what you want, is where this comparison lives.

CliftonStrengths and the five energies are often put in the same bucket of assessments, which is why coaches sometimes feel they have to choose one. They do not really overlap. One maps talent, the other maps appetite. Knowing which question your client is actually asking is worth more than either instrument on its own.

What CliftonStrengths measures well

CliftonStrengths sorts a person's answers into thirty-four talent themes and returns a ranked list. It is the product of a long research programme, it has an enormous body of organisational use behind it, and its language is deliberately positive. The output tells you where someone is likely to learn fast, contribute naturally and feel competent.

That last part is why it works so well in teams. A manager can look at a list of themes and immediately think about how to shape a project, who to pair with whom, where a person will be useful this quarter. It gives people permission to name what they are good at without sounding boastful, which is a genuinely useful social function.

The limitation is built into the design. A strength is a description of capability, not of direction. It does not tell you whether the person wants to keep using it, whether using it has become a trap, or whether the thing they are best at is the thing quietly exhausting them. A ranked list has no place to put ambivalence.

What the five energies ask instead

The five energies are a lens for conversation rather than a fixed type. Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water name different ways of moving: growth and direction, connection and visibility, steadiness and care, precision and standards, depth and inwardness. A read gives you a dominant tendency and, more usefully, what that tendency tends to cost when it runs unchecked.

The framework is built on a birth date and, where available, a small set of body-composition numbers. It is worth being plain about what that means: this is a reflection tool for preparing a conversation, not a clinical or diagnostic instrument, and not a psychometric test with a normative sample. What a coach receives is small by design, three topics the client may be circling, two angles to open them from, and one blind spot.

Notice that none of those outputs are compliments. There is no ranked list of what the person is good at. The read is trying to surface what has not been said yet, which is a different job from naming what has already been proven.

The client who is good at something they no longer want

This is the case where the two tools part company most clearly. Someone with a strong responsibility theme and a long record of being the person who holds everything together may be excellent at care. They may also be deeply tired of being the person who holds everything together. A strengths conversation, run carelessly, will invite them to do more of it with better framing.

An energy read tends to land somewhere else. Earth that has been overused shows up as steadiness that has quietly become an inability to say no, or as a person who has become so reliable that nobody thinks to ask what they need. The useful question is not how do we build on this strength. It is what would you do with a week in which nobody needed you.

Neither tool is wrong here. One is describing a capability the person genuinely has. The other is pointing at a cost the person has stopped noticing. A first session usually needs the second question more than the first.

How to hold both in one conversation

If a client brings their strengths list, treat it as their own account of themselves and do not argue with it. It is often the first time they have had neutral language for something they were taught to play down. Start there, let them tell you which theme feels most like them, and listen for the one they describe with the least energy.

Then use the energy read as a hypothesis rather than a finding. A workable opening is: I have a sense the part that is most reliable about you is also the part you would most like a rest from. Does that land, or am I reading it wrong. The invitation to correct you matters as much as the observation. Their correction is usually where the real session begins.

Keep the two outputs in different registers when you write your notes. Strengths are what the person can do. Energies are a guess about what they are protecting, or reaching for, or avoiding. Confusing the two is how a coach ends up telling a capable person to be more capable.

  • CliftonStrengths: capability, talent themes, ranked, strong for team design and role shaping
  • Five energies: appetite and cost, a small set of topics, built for intake before session one
  • Use together: start with the language the client brought, then ask what that language leaves out

Choosing a first tool

If the work is role design, team composition, or helping someone name what they contribute, CliftonStrengths is the better instrument. It is shared, positive and easy to build a workshop around. If the work is a person deciding what to do next, or noticing a pattern they keep repeating, the five energies give you more to open with.

For a ten-minute intake before a first session, a short set of questions plus an energy read will usually produce a more useful meeting than a full strengths debrief. There is not enough time to do the debrief justice, and the debrief tends to fill the space that listening needed.

The practical answer for most coaches is to keep both available and decide from the client's question, not from habit. What do I do well is a different conversation from what do I actually want, and the first session is usually where the second one finally gets asked.
