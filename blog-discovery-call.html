The Discovery Call That Decides Everything

2026-10-10 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

You have probably had this experience: a discovery call feels great. The prospect laughs at your jokes, nods at your insights, says all the right things. You hang up believing you have a new client. Then silence. No signed agreement, no reply to your follow-up email. What happened?

The discovery call is not a performance. It is a structured conversation that either builds a clear bridge to your paid work or reveals that no bridge exists. Most coaches treat it as a friendly chat followed by a pitch. That approach wastes time, creates mismatched engagements, and leaves both people frustrated. A better call has three parts: questions that surface the real work, boundaries around what you promise, and one final question that tells you whether to quote a price at all.

Why most discovery calls fail before they start

The typical coaching sales call begins with rapport building, moves to a description of your program, and ends with a price. The prospect is passive. You are doing all the work. By the time you mention your fee, you are hoping they say yes. That is not a discovery call. That is a pitch with a friendly face.

The failure is structural. You have not asked enough to know whether this person is a good fit for your approach, your style, and your availability. You have not heard them describe what they have already tried. You have not learned what happens if nothing changes. Without that information, any price you quote is a guess. Worse, you may attract a client who needs a different kind of support entirely.

A discovery call that works feels less like a sales conversation and more like a diagnostic interview. You are not trying to convince. You are trying to understand. The prospect should leave the call knowing exactly what you do, how you work, and whether they want to continue. You should leave knowing the same.

What to ask: four questions that surface the real work

Start with the present. Ask: What is happening right now that made you book this call? Let them talk. Do not interrupt with your own story. Listen for specifics. A prospect who says I am stressed is different from one who says I have been offered a promotion but I am terrified of managing my former peers. The second person has a concrete situation you can work with.

Next, ask about history. What have you already tried to change this? This question does two things. It shows respect for their effort. It also reveals whether they are looking for a magic solution or a working partnership. If they have tried three different approaches and can describe what did not fit, they are likely ready for a tailored engagement. If they have tried nothing and want you to fix it, that is a warning sign.

Then ask about stakes. What happens if nothing changes in the next six months? This is not about fear. It is about clarity. A client who can name the cost of inaction is usually more committed. A client who shrugs and says probably nothing will be harder to motivate. You are not judging them. You are gathering data about their readiness.

Finally, ask about support. What kind of support has worked for you in the past? Some people thrive with weekly accountability. Others need space to reflect and only want a monthly check-in. If you only offer one format, you need to know whether it matches their history. If it does not, say so. That honesty saves you both from a difficult engagement.

  • What is happening right now that made you book this call?
  • What have you already tried to change this?
  • What happens if nothing changes in the next six months?
  • What kind of support has worked for you in the past?

What not to promise: boundaries that protect the work

Coaches get into trouble when they promise outcomes they cannot control. You cannot promise a promotion, a reconciled relationship, or a new business revenue target. You can promise your attention, your process, and your honesty. That is it. Anything more is a setup for disappointment.

Avoid language like I will help you double your income or you will feel confident in three sessions. Instead, say: I will help you clarify what you want and build a plan to move toward it. I will hold you accountable to the steps you choose. I will tell you the truth, even when it is uncomfortable. These are promises you can keep.

This matters especially during the discovery call. If a prospect asks, Can you help me get a new job? the honest answer is: I can help you get clear on what kind of job you want and how to present yourself. I cannot control who hires you. That answer builds trust. It also filters out people who want a magician rather than a coach.

One more boundary: do not promise a specific number of sessions before you understand the situation. A six-session package might be perfect for one person and far too short for another. Say: I usually work with clients for three to six months. After we start, we will decide together how long makes sense. This keeps the engagement flexible and honest.

The one question that tells you whether to quote a price

After you have asked your questions and set your boundaries, there is one final question. It is simple, and it will save you hours of follow-up. Ask: On a scale of one to ten, how ready are you to begin this work right now?

If the answer is eight, nine, or ten, you can move to price. They are ready. They have the urgency, the history, and the willingness to partner. You can quote your fee with confidence, knowing that you are offering something they actually want.

If the answer is seven or below, do not quote a price. Instead, ask: What would need to happen for that number to be higher? Their answer tells you whether this is a timing issue, a budget issue, or a fit issue. Sometimes the answer is I need to talk to my partner. Sometimes it is I am not sure I can commit the time. Sometimes it is I think I need a therapist, not a coach. All of those are useful. None of them require you to pitch.

This question works because it puts the decision in their hands. You are not chasing them. You are not convincing them. You are simply asking them to name their own readiness. If they are not ready, you can part as friends and leave the door open. If they are ready, you have a clear signal to proceed. Either way, you have protected your time and your integrity.

Putting it into practice on your next call

Before your next discovery call, write down the four questions and the readiness question. Keep them in front of you. Resist the urge to fill silence. Let the prospect think. Their pauses often contain the most important information.

After the call, take five minutes to write a short summary. What did they say they wanted? What have they tried? What is at stake? What is their readiness number? If you decide to send a proposal, tailor it to their words, not your standard template. If you decide not to send one, send a brief note thanking them and suggesting a better-fit resource. That small act of honesty often leads to referrals later.

The discovery call is not a test of your sales ability. It is a test of your listening. When you listen well, you do not need to convince anyone. You simply need to recognize the right fit and walk away from the wrong one. That is how a coaching business grows without burnout.
