How to Tell a Coaching Client Something Unwelcome

2026-10-17 · 6 min read · by Brian Zhang

There is a moment in coaching when you see something the client has not yet seen, and you know that naming it will land awkwardly. Maybe it is the fourth time they have described the same meeting with the same manager. Maybe their pattern is visible in the room before it is visible to them. You have a choice: keep circling, or say the thing.

Saying the thing is part of the work. But how you say it matters more than whether you are brave enough to say it. In this article I want to give you a simple structure that keeps honest feedback usable, and then show you four sentences that reliably turn a difficult conversation into a shutdown. I have used these in my own practice, and I have watched coaches use them well and badly. The difference is rarely courage. It is craft.

Start with the pattern, not the person

The first move is to separate the pattern from the person. If you say, 'You always avoid conflict,' the client hears a verdict about their character. If you say, 'I notice that when the topic of your sister comes up, you move the conversation to work,' they hear an observation about a sequence. The second version is easier to stay with because it is specific and it belongs to the session, not to their identity.

A coach I work with had a client who kept saying yes to extra projects and then arriving exhausted. She had tried naming the exhaustion and got nowhere. She changed the entry point: 'In the last three sessions, every time you describe a request from your director, you say yes in the first sentence and explain the cost in the third.' The client went quiet, then laughed. That was the pattern becoming visible without anyone being accused of anything.

Keep the observation tied to evidence the client can check. 'I have heard you say yes before you finish reading the email' is checkable. 'You are a people-pleaser' is not. The first invites curiosity. The second invites defence.

Name the cost in their language, not yours

Once the pattern is on the table, the next step is to connect it to something the client already cares about. This is where many honest conversations go wrong: the coach names a cost that matters to the coach, not to the client. You might see the cost as lost sleep or a stalled career. They might experience it as letting the team down or being seen as difficult.

Use their words. If they have said, 'I just want to get through the week without dropping anything,' then the cost is dropping things. Try: 'When you say yes to the extra project, the thing that gets dropped is the one you told me you wanted to protect, the Friday afternoon with your son.' That is not a lecture. It is their own priority reflected back.

This step also gives you a way to check whether the observation is fair. If you name a cost and the client says, 'Actually, that part does not bother me,' you have learned something. You can adjust. Honest feedback is not a fixed script; it is a hypothesis you are willing to revise.

Ask before you interpret

The third move is the one coaches skip most often. After you name the pattern and the cost, ask a genuine question before offering your interpretation. 'What do you make of that?' or 'Does that match how it feels from the inside?' This does two things. It gives the client agency in the conversation, and it gives you information about whether you are on target.

I watched a session where a coach said, 'You seem to take responsibility for everyone's mood, and I wonder if that is why you are tired.' Then she stopped. The client sat forward and said, 'No, it is not their mood. It is that I think if I do not manage it, they will leave.' That was a more precise pattern, and it came from the client. The coach's observation opened the door; the question let the client walk through it.

If the client says, 'I do not see it that way,' resist the urge to argue. You can say, 'That is useful. What do you see instead?' You are not there to win the point. You are there to keep the conversation honest and open.

The four sentences that shut it down

Now the part that is easier to demonstrate than describe. These four sentences are common, well-intentioned, and they close the door. I have said all of them. The point is not to feel bad about that; it is to recognise them when they are forming.

The first is, 'I am just going to be honest with you.' It sounds brave, but it signals that what follows is a performance of honesty rather than an invitation. The second is, 'You always do this.' The word always turns an observation into a character judgement. The third is, 'Other people have noticed too.' This recruits an audience the client cannot see or question. The fourth is, 'I think you know this already.' It puts the client in a position where disagreeing means admitting they are in denial.

Each of these sentences takes the conversation out of the room and puts it on the client. The alternative is not softer; it is more specific. Instead of 'I am just going to be honest,' try 'Can I tell you what I noticed?' Instead of 'You always do this,' try 'In the last two sessions, this came up twice.' Instead of 'Other people have noticed,' try 'I noticed.' Instead of 'You know this already,' try 'What is your sense of it?'

  • 'I am just going to be honest with you' - performative, invites defence.
  • 'You always do this' - turns a pattern into a verdict.
  • 'Other people have noticed too' - recruits an audience the client cannot check.
  • 'I think you know this already' - makes disagreement feel like denial.

A worked example from a real session

A client, a senior operations manager, came to coaching because she wanted to delegate more. Session after session, she described rewriting her team's work late at night. I had named the pattern twice and got polite agreement and no change. The third time, I used the structure.

I said, 'I notice that when you describe a piece of work your team has done, you mention the version you would have produced. That has happened in each of the last four sessions.' Then I named the cost in her language: 'You told me the thing you want is to stop working evenings. When you rewrite, the evening goes.' Then I asked, 'What do you make of that?'

She was quiet for a moment and then said, 'I think I do not trust them.' That was the real pattern, and it had not been visible to her until the observation was specific enough to check. We spent the next twenty minutes on trust rather than delegation. If I had said, 'You are a control freak,' we would have spent the session repairing the relationship instead.

Keeping the relationship intact afterwards

An honest observation lands better when the client knows the relationship can hold it. That means after the conversation, you return to ordinary ground. Ask how the session felt. Ask what they want to take with them. Do not leave them sitting in the discomfort without a way back to their own agency.

It also means being willing to be wrong out loud. 'I might be off. Tell me what I am missing' is not a weakening of your position. It is a demonstration that the observation is about their pattern, not about your authority. Clients remember that. They are more likely to stay honest with you when they have seen you stay honest with them.

The structure is not a formula for getting it right every time. It is a way to keep the conversation usable when the subject is unwelcome. Name the pattern, name the cost in their language, ask before you interpret, and watch your own sentences for the four that close the door. That is most of the craft.
