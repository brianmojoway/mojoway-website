Difficult Clients: Five Friction Patterns and One De-Escalating Move

2026-11-03 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

Every coach has one. The client who arrives late, sighs through the session, and answers every question with a flat 'I don't know.' Or the one who talks for forty minutes straight and then asks why you never give them anything useful. We label them difficult, and the label feels accurate in the moment. But in the MOJOWAY framework, the friction is rarely about a person being inherently hard to work with. It is usually a mismatch between the energy they bring and the energy the session is asking of them.

This guide is not about diagnosing anyone. It is a reflection tool for you, the coach, to notice the pattern in the room and adjust your own stance first. Below are five friction patterns, each with a worked example and the one move that tends to lower the temperature. You will recognise at least two of them from your own practice this month.

Pattern one: the over-explainer (Wood energy)

Wood energy is about direction, growth and forward motion. When it is out of balance in a coaching session, it often shows up as a client who cannot stop explaining. They give you the full context, the backstory, the three possible interpretations of their manager's email. You ask a simple question and get a seven-minute answer. It feels like resistance, but it is usually momentum that has nowhere to land.

Worked example: a marketing director named Priya books six sessions to 'get clarity on her next career move.' In session two she spends twenty-five minutes describing the org chart, her boss's communication style, and a podcast she heard. You notice you have stopped breathing. That is your cue. The Wood energy is not trying to avoid the work. It is trying to find a direction and keeps accelerating because nothing has caught it yet.

The move: name the momentum and give it a container. Say, 'You have given me a lot of useful context. I want to make sure we use it. Can we take the next ten minutes and land on one question you want to answer by the end of this session?' You are not shutting the energy down. You are giving it a bank to flow into. Priya will usually exhale, pick a question, and do more focused work in ten minutes than in the previous twenty-five.

Pattern two: the slow-burner (Fire energy)

Fire energy is about connection, visibility and warmth. When it is under-fuelled, the client can seem flat, disengaged or quietly resentful. They answer in short sentences. They do not laugh at your jokes. They say 'I suppose so' in a way that makes you want to check your notes. It is easy to read this as the client not wanting to be there. More often, the Fire is low because the client does not feel seen yet.

Worked example: a newly promoted team lead, Marcus, has been told by his company that he needs 'more executive presence.' In session one he is monosyllabic. You ask about his team and he shrugs. Then you ask, 'What is the part of this job you actually like?' He pauses, then talks for four minutes about mentoring a junior analyst. His posture changes. The Fire was there. It just had not been invited.

The move: ask a warmth question before a challenge question. Before you go anywhere near the development area, find the one thing in the client's week that lit them up. Name it back to them specifically. You are not being soft. You are building the relational heat that makes the harder work possible. Marcus will tolerate a stretch assignment conversation far better once he knows you have noticed what he cares about.

Pattern three: the caretaker (Earth energy)

Earth energy is about nurture, stability and holding things together. In a coaching session, an Earth-heavy client often takes care of you. They ask how your week has been. They apologise for taking up your time. They bring the conversation back to your workload. It looks like politeness. Underneath, it is often a way of staying busy so they do not have to be the focus.

Worked example: a nonprofit director, Helen, has booked coaching because she is 'burned out and not sure why.' In session three she spends ten minutes asking about your practice and whether you are busy. You notice you are reassuring her. That is the pattern. Helen is more comfortable holding the room than being held by it.

The move: receive the care out loud and redirect it. Say, 'Thank you for asking about my week. I am going to take that as a sign you are good at looking after people. I want to spend the next twenty minutes looking after you.' Then ask a direct question about her own need. It will feel slightly uncomfortable, which is the point. Helen does not need another person she has to manage. She needs a session where she is not the host.

Pattern four: the perfectionist (Metal energy)

Metal energy is about standards, clarity and discernment. When it tightens, the client becomes exacting, self-critical and hard to satisfy. They want the right answer. They want the framework to be rigorous. They push back on your questions because the questions are not precise enough. It can feel like they are auditing you. Often they are auditing themselves, more harshly than they would ever audit you.

Worked example: a senior lawyer, Daniel, wants to work on 'decision-making under pressure.' Every time you offer a reflection, he finds the flaw in it. 'That is a bit generic.' 'I am not sure that applies to my situation.' You start over-preparing for sessions. That is the tell. The Metal energy has recruited you into its quality-control department.

The move: agree with the standard, then lower the stakes. Say, 'You are right that this is not a precise answer. I am not trying to give you one yet. I am trying to notice what comes up when you do not have one.' You are not defending your competence. You are inviting him to tolerate a slightly less polished process. Daniel will usually relax once he sees you are not asking him to accept something shoddy. You are asking him to stay in the messy middle, which is where the useful work happens.

Bullets for noticing the Metal tighten in yourself:

You notice you are justifying your method or your credentials.

You feel a flicker of irritation at a 'yes, but' response.

You start editing your questions before you ask them.

Pattern five: the deflector (Water energy)

Water energy is about adaptability, depth and flow. When it is overactive, the client adapts to every question without landing anywhere. They agree with your reflection, then move to the next topic. They cry and then laugh and then change the subject. They are not being evasive on purpose. They are moving with the current, and the current is fast.

Worked example: a product manager, Sam, has had three sessions and you could not tell anyone what he is actually working on. He has insight after insight. He thanks you warmly at the end of each session. Nothing changes. You feel oddly unmoored afterwards, as if you had a deep conversation with someone who was never quite there.

The move: slow the water down with one concrete anchor. At the end of the session, ask, 'What is the one thing you will do differently before we speak again, and when will you do it?' Write it down together. Do not accept a feeling or an insight as the answer. Ask for a behaviour and a time. Sam will resist slightly, then name something specific. That specificity is the bank that holds the water. Without it, the insight evaporates by Thursday.

Using the map without labelling the client

The point of this field guide is not to sort your clients into five boxes and treat them accordingly. It is to give you a faster read on your own reaction. When you feel yourself over-explaining, over-preparing, over-reassuring or over-managing, that is the signal. The friction pattern is usually visible in your behaviour before it is visible in theirs.

A simple practice: after each session, write one line about the energy you noticed and one line about what you did in response. Over a month, you will see your own defaults. Most coaches have one pattern they find easiest and one they find hardest. Knowing which is which is more useful than any client label.

MOJOWAY is a reflection framework, not a clinical or diagnostic tool. It will not tell you what is wrong with a client, and it is not designed to. It will help you notice the temperature in the room and adjust your own stance. That is usually enough to turn a difficult session into a workable one.
