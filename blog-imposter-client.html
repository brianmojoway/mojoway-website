Coaching the High Achiever Who Feels Like a Fraud

2026-10-15 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

You are on a call with a client who has just been promoted to a role they have wanted for years. Their track record is excellent. Their peers respect them. And yet they spend most of the session explaining why the promotion was probably a mistake. They are waiting for someone to tap them on the shoulder and say there has been a mix-up. If you coach high performers, you have met this client. They are not lacking evidence of competence. They are lacking permission to trust it.

The instinct for many coaches is to reassure. You point out the wins. You remind them of the feedback. You say, quite reasonably, that they earned this. And the client nods politely and returns next week with the same doubt, now slightly more polished. This article looks at what is actually happening for these clients, what tends to make things worse, and why reassurance, however well-intentioned, is usually the least useful thing you can offer.

Why reassurance backfires with self-doubt clients

Reassurance feels kind, but it quietly moves the conversation in the wrong direction. When you tell a client they deserve the role, you become the judge and they become the defendant. They now have to either accept your verdict or argue against it. Many high achievers argue. They are good at arguing. They have spent years building a case for why they are not quite ready, and your reassurance gives that case fresh material to work with.

There is a second problem. Reassurance implies the doubt is a factual error that can be corrected with better information. But for most of these clients, the doubt is not a conclusion they reached from evidence. It is a background condition they have learned to work around. They do not need more evidence. They need a different relationship with the doubt itself.

  • Reassurance positions you as the authority on their worth, which they will resist.
  • It treats doubt as a knowledge gap rather than a pattern.
  • It can make the client feel unheard, even when your intent is supportive.
  • It often ends the exploration just as it was getting useful.

What is actually happening for the high achiever

Take a client we will call Maya, a director at a mid-sized firm who was recently asked to lead a division of forty people. In session, she described a recurring moment: walking into the leadership meeting, looking around the table, and feeling like she had wandered into a room she was not qualified to enter. She could list her achievements without hesitation. The feeling did not shift.

What Maya was describing is not a lack of confidence in her skills. It is a mismatch between her internal sense of self and the external role she now occupies. She has a story about who she is, built over years, and the new role does not fit that story yet. The doubt is not a bug. It is a signal that her identity has not caught up with her position.

This is why reassurance bounces off. You are speaking to the external role. She is living in the internal story. Until those two things are in conversation with each other, the doubt will keep returning, no matter how many wins you list.

What helps: curiosity over correction

The most useful move is to stop trying to fix the doubt and start getting curious about it. When Maya said she felt like a fraud, the coach did not say, but look at your results. The coach asked, what does the fraud part of you think it is protecting you from? That question changed the room. Maya paused. She had never been asked that before.

What emerged was that the doubt was doing a job. It kept her preparing harder than anyone else. It stopped her from becoming complacent. It was, in its own strange way, a form of care. Once she could see that, she could decide whether she still needed it at this level, or whether it was now costing her more than it gave.

This is the shift from reassurance to inquiry. You are not validating the doubt. You are treating it as information. The client gets to be the expert on their own experience, which is a far more durable position than accepting your reassurance.

  • Ask what the doubt is trying to protect.
  • Ask when the doubt first showed up in their career.
  • Ask what the doubt costs them now that it did not cost them before.
  • Ask what they would do if the doubt were simply present, not decisive.

Working with the Five Elements lens

In the MOJOWAY framework, we look at patterns through the lens of the Five Elements. A client like Maya often presents with a strong Metal quality: high standards, precision, a tendency to measure themselves against an internal ideal that no real person can meet. Metal is valuable. It is also exhausting when it has no counterbalance.

What Maya needed was not less Metal but more Water, the element of flow, trust and allowing. In practice, that looked like small experiments: speaking in a meeting without over-preparing, sharing a half-formed idea, noticing what happened when she did not have the perfect answer. Each experiment gave her a data point that her internal story had been excluding.

This is not a diagnostic tool and it is not a personality test. It is a reflection framework. It gives the client a language for the pattern without making the pattern the whole story. Maya is not a Metal type. She is a person who, in this season, is leaning heavily on one quality and missing another.

What makes it worse

Beyond reassurance, there are a few other moves that tend to deepen the hole. One is sharing your own imposter feelings as a way to normalise theirs. It can help briefly, but it often shifts the focus to you and leaves the client with less room to explore their own experience.

Another is jumping to action too quickly. Sending a client away with a confidence-building exercise before they have understood what the doubt is doing can feel like being managed rather than met. The exercise may even work, but the underlying pattern stays untouched.

A third is treating the doubt as something to eliminate. High achievers often hear this as, your instincts are wrong, which is the opposite of helpful. The goal is not to remove the doubt. The goal is to stop letting it make the decisions.

A simple way to open the conversation

If you are sitting with a client who does not believe they deserve the role, try opening with something like this: I am not going to try to convince you that you deserve it. I am more interested in what happens inside you when you imagine that you do. Then wait. Let the silence do some of the work.

What usually follows is not a list of reasons they are unqualified. It is a quieter, more honest reflection about what it would mean to take up space, to be seen, to be responsible for something that matters. That is the conversation worth having. It is slower than reassurance. It is also the one that tends to stay with the client long after the session ends.
