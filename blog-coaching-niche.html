Why Naming a Specific Client Makes Coaching Easier

2026-10-21 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

Most coaches I talk to are not short on skill. They are short on clarity about who they are for. Their website says they work with leaders, founders, creatives, and anyone feeling stuck. Their discovery calls are a series of hopeful questions. Their marketing feels like shouting into a crowded room. The work is good, but the business feels heavier than it should.

Naming a specific client changes that. Not because it is a marketing trick, but because it gives your thinking somewhere to land. When you know who you are speaking to, your examples get sharper, your questions get better, and your referrals start to make sense. This article is about how to choose that client without feeling like you are slamming the door on good people, and how to test the choice with real conversations before you commit.

A specific client makes the rest of the business simpler

Consider a coach I worked with, let us call her Maya. She described her practice as helping professionals navigate transition. Her website was elegant and vague. On calls, she spent the first twenty minutes working out what kind of transition the person was in. Then she would adapt her language, her examples, and her framework on the fly. She was good at it, which was part of the problem. Every call cost her more energy than it needed to.

When Maya started describing her client as a mid-level manager returning to work after a career break, several things changed at once. Her website copy stopped hedging. Her intake questions got more precise. She could describe her work in one sentence at a networking event. Referrals became easier because people finally knew who to send her way. She did not turn away other clients. She simply stopped building her business around the possibility of everyone.

  • Your examples get concrete because you are drawing on a recognizable situation.
  • Your questions get sharper because you know what usually matters to this person.
  • Your referrals get easier because other people can repeat who you help.
  • Your own confidence grows because you are no longer improvising your identity on every call.

Choosing a niche is not the same as refusing people

The fear is real: if I name a niche, I will have to say no to good people. In practice, naming a niche is a statement of emphasis, not a gate. You can describe who you are best suited to help and still work with others when the fit is right. What changes is the default. Instead of asking every potential client to figure out whether they belong, you tell them where you shine.

Think of a physiotherapist who specializes in runners. They still treat a cyclist with a bad back. But their waiting room, their intake form, and their reputation are built around runners. That focus does not shrink their practice. It makes them the person runners recommend to each other. A coaching niche works the same way. It gives people a reason to remember you and a reason to refer you.

How to choose a niche that fits you

A good niche sits at the intersection of three things: people you understand, problems you have seen up close, and work you are willing to talk about for years. Start with the clients you already have. Which conversations leave you energized rather than drained? Which problems do you solve almost without thinking? Those are signals, not accidents.

Then get specific enough to be useful. Helping people with confidence is a theme. Helping new team leaders who were promoted from technical roles and now have to run their first performance review is a niche. The second version gives you something to say, something to test, and something a stranger can repeat. If you are unsure, write three candidate niches and notice which one you can explain to a friend in under thirty seconds without reaching for abstractions.

  • Look at your last ten clients and note the pattern in the problems they brought.
  • Ask which conversations you would happily have ten more times this month.
  • Write your niche as a person plus a situation, not a feeling or a virtue.
  • Check that you can describe it in one plain sentence without jargon.

Test the niche with real conversations before you commit

A niche is a hypothesis, not a vow. Before you rebuild your website around it, have five to eight conversations with people who match the description. Not sales calls. Short, honest conversations where you ask what their week actually looks like, what they have tried, and what they wish someone would help them with. Listen for the language they use. If they describe the problem differently than you do, borrow their words.

One coach I know tested a niche around first-time managers by offering four free conversations to people in her network who had recently been promoted. She did not pitch. She asked questions and took notes. By the fourth call she had heard the same sentence three times: I did not realize how much of this job is managing people, not the work. That sentence became the first line of her website. The test cost her a few hours and gave her more clarity than a month of planning.

You can also test in smaller ways. Change the headline on your profile and watch who responds. Offer a short workshop to a specific group and see who shows up. Ask two trusted colleagues to describe who they think you help. Each of these is a low-stakes experiment that gives you real information. Commit when the pattern is consistent, not when the idea is clever.

What to do when a client outside the niche arrives

You will still get inquiries from people outside your chosen focus. That is not a failure of the niche. It is a chance to be honest. You can say: my work is mostly with new managers, and I am happy to talk about whether this is a fit. Sometimes it is. Sometimes you will refer them to someone better placed. Either way, the conversation is clearer because you are not pretending to be everything to everyone.

The niche is there to help you choose, not to make the choice for you. It gives you a default, a language, and a reason for people to remember you. It does not remove your judgment. Coaches who name a specific client tend to find that the work gets more focused, the marketing gets easier, and the clients who do arrive feel more like a fit. That is not a promise about outcomes. It is simply what tends to happen when you stop hedging and start describing the work you are actually here to do.

Ready to name your client? Start with one conversation.
