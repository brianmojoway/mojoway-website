When Clients Text Between Sessions: Boundaries That Read as Care

2026-10-11 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

It's 8:47 on a Tuesday night. Your phone lights up: a client you saw this morning has sent a three-paragraph text about a conflict with their manager. You feel the pull to respond immediately. You also feel a flicker of resentment because this is the fourth time this month. You want to be supportive, but you also need a life. What do you do?

This is one of the most common boundary questions in coaching. The answer isn't a rigid rule. It's a clear agreement that you design before the first session, revisit when needed, and communicate in a way that feels like care rather than distance. In this article, we'll walk through what to allow, what to charge for, and exactly how to word it so your clients feel held, not pushed away.

Why between-session contact feels so loaded

Coaching is a relationship, and relationships have rhythms. When a client texts you between sessions, they're often reaching for connection, reassurance, or a quick sense of progress. That's human. But without a clear agreement, you become the default container for their anxiety, and that's not sustainable for either of you.

Many coaches swing between two extremes: total availability (answering at all hours, which leads to burnout) or total silence (ignoring messages until the next session, which can feel cold and confusing). Neither serves the client or the coach. The middle path is a defined channel with defined response times and defined scope.

Think of it like a well-run classroom. Students know when office hours are, how to submit questions, and what counts as urgent. The structure doesn't make the teacher less caring. It makes the care reliable.

What to allow: three tiers of contact

Before you decide what to charge for, decide what you're willing to offer as part of your standard fee. Most coaches land on a three-tier model. Tier one is logistics: scheduling changes, time zone confusion, or a quick note that they'll be five minutes late. These are fine to handle briefly and don't require deep coaching.

Tier two is a brief check-in. This might be a short text or voice note where the client shares a win or a stuck point. You can acknowledge it without diving into coaching. A simple, 'I see this, and I'm glad you sent it. Let's unpack it on Thursday' is often enough. It honors the moment without turning it into a session.

Tier three is coaching between sessions. This is where you're actively reflecting, asking questions, or helping them work through a situation in real time. That's your professional service, and it deserves a separate agreement. When you separate logistics from coaching, you can be generous with the first and clear about the second.

  • Tier 1: Logistics only (scheduling, links, brief confirmations). No charge, quick reply.
  • Tier 2: Brief acknowledgment (a win, a stuck point). Reply within one business day, no coaching.
  • Tier 3: Active coaching between sessions. Requires a separate agreement and fee.

What to charge for: packaging between-session support

If you enjoy offering between-session coaching, you can make it a paid add-on. For example, one coach I know offers a 'mid-week pulse' package: one 15-minute voice-note exchange or a scheduled 20-minute call for an additional fee. She frames it as an intentional container, not an open-ended lifeline.

Another approach is to build a limited number of 'emergency' sessions into your higher-tier packages. Say, two 20-minute calls per month, to be used when the client is in a high-stakes moment. You set the price so it reflects your time and the value of immediate support. The key is that it's defined, limited, and agreed upon in advance.

Charging for between-session coaching isn't greedy. It's honest. It acknowledges that your time and expertise have value, and it prevents the slow resentment that builds when you feel taken advantage of. Clients respect clear pricing. What they don't respect is vagueness followed by withdrawal.

How to word the boundary so it reads as care

The language matters as much as the policy. Instead of saying, 'I don't respond to texts between sessions,' try, 'I want you to have support that's reliable, so here's how we'll stay connected between sessions.' Then describe the channel and the response time. For example: 'You can text me for scheduling or a quick win. I'll reply within one business day. For deeper coaching, we'll use our sessions or a paid between-session call.'

Another caring frame is to name the why. 'I've found that when I coach over text, I can't give you my full attention, and you deserve better than a rushed reply. So I hold coaching for our sessions, where we have space and focus.' This positions the boundary as a commitment to quality, not a rejection.

Finally, give them an alternative. If they're in a hard moment and you're not available, what should they do? You might offer a grounding practice, a journaling prompt, or a reminder to use their support network. This shows you've thought about their experience, not just your own schedule.

  • Name the channel: 'Text me for logistics or a quick win.'
  • Name the response time: 'I reply within one business day.'
  • Name the alternative: 'For deeper support, we'll use our session or a paid call.'

Putting it in your coaching agreement

Your coaching agreement is the natural home for these boundaries. Don't bury it in fine print. Have a short, plain-English section called 'Between-session contact.' Include what's included, what's not, response times, and how to request additional support. Review it at the start of the engagement, and revisit it if the pattern shifts.

When you present it, do so with warmth. 'I want to make sure you always know how to reach me and what to expect. Here's what I've found works best for my clients.' Then pause and ask if they have questions. This turns a policy into a conversation, and it gives them a chance to voice their needs.

If a client pushes back, listen. Sometimes they're anxious, and they need reassurance that you're not disappearing. You can say, 'I'm not going anywhere. I'm just making our contact predictable so you can trust it.' That's the difference between a boundary that feels like a wall and one that feels like a handrail.

What to do when a client crosses the line

It will happen. A client will text you a long, emotional message at 11 p.m., or send five messages in a row. In that moment, you have a choice. You can ignore it and hope they get the hint, or you can respond with a brief, kind redirection. The second option is almost always better.

Try something like: 'I can hear how much this is weighing on you. I want to give this the attention it deserves, so let's put it at the top of our next session. If you'd like to talk sooner, we can book a between-session call.' This acknowledges their pain, reinforces the boundary, and offers a path forward.

Then, in your next session, name the pattern. 'I noticed you texted a few times this week. I'm glad you reached out. Let's talk about how we can make sure you feel supported without either of us getting overwhelmed.' This keeps the relationship honest and gives you both a chance to adjust.
