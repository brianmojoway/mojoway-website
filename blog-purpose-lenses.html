Ikigai and the Five Energies: Two Lenses on Purpose

2026-10-02 · 5 min read · by Brian Zhang

Purpose coaching often reaches for ikigai, the Japanese concept that maps what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for. It is a useful lens, but it can leave clients stuck in their heads, circling a Venn diagram without a felt sense of direction. The five elements offer a different entry point: a way to notice which energy is moving in a client's life right now, and which one has gone quiet.

This article shows how to use both lenses in a single conversation. You do not need to choose between them. Ikigai gives you the map. The five elements give you the weather. Together, they help a client see not just where they want to go, but what is moving them there, and what might be blocking the way.

Ikigai as a map, not a destination

Ikigai is often drawn as four overlapping circles. The sweet spot sits in the middle. But in practice, few clients arrive with all four circles neatly aligned. They might love the work but cannot pay rent. Or they are paid well but feel hollow. The map is useful because it names the tension, not because it resolves it.

Use ikigai as a diagnostic, not a verdict. Ask your client to rate each circle from one to ten. Then ask which rating surprises them most. You are not looking for a perfect ten. You are looking for the gap that carries the most energy, the one that makes them lean forward or look away.

  • What I love: activities that absorb me
  • What I am good at: skills others notice
  • What the world needs: problems that pull me
  • What I can be paid for: value someone will exchange

The five energies as a lived check-in

The five elements, or five energies, are a Daoist framework for noticing how life moves. Each energy has a quality: Wood plans and pushes forward. Fire connects and expresses. Earth holds and nourishes. Metal refines and lets go. Water reflects and rests. None is better than another. They are phases, not personality types.

In a coaching session, you can use them as a simple check-in. Ask your client: which of these feels most alive in you this week? Which feels depleted? You are not diagnosing anything. You are giving them a vocabulary for their own experience, one that changes as their circumstances change.

Where the two lenses meet

Ikigai asks what. The five energies ask how. A client might say they want to leave their corporate job to become a coach. That is an ikigai statement. But when you ask which energy is behind it, they might say Wood: a pushing, forward-driving energy that wants to break through. Or Water: a quiet, reflective pull that has been gathering for months.

The same ikigai goal feels different depending on the energy behind it. A Wood-driven move may need a plan and a deadline. A Water-driven move may need rest, journaling, and permission to not know yet. If you treat them the same, you risk pushing a client who needs to pause, or waiting on a client who needs to act.

  • Wood: vision, boundaries, forward motion
  • Fire: connection, visibility, joy
  • Earth: stability, care, follow-through
  • Metal: discernment, quality, release
  • Water: reflection, rest, trust

A worked example: Maya's next chapter

Maya is a marketing director who wants more meaning at work. Her ikigai circles show high scores for what she loves and what she is good at, but low scores for what the world needs and what she can be paid for. She feels stuck. When you ask which energy is most alive, she says Fire: she loves connecting people and ideas. When you ask which is quiet, she says Water: she has not stopped to reflect in years.

Instead of jumping to a career change, you design a small experiment. She will spend two weeks in Water energy: thirty minutes of unstructured reflection three times a week, no agenda. Then she will bring one Fire insight to her current role, perhaps mentoring a junior colleague or pitching a project that connects two teams. The goal is not to fix her ikigai. It is to let the missing energy speak.

After two weeks, Maya reports that the reflection time felt uncomfortable at first, then clarifying. She realizes she does not need a new job. She needs more Earth energy at work: steadier routines, clearer boundaries, and a sense of follow-through. The ikigai map did not change. The energy check-in changed what she did next.

How to run the conversation

Start with ikigai. Ask the four questions. Let the client talk. Notice where they light up and where they go flat. Then introduce the five energies as a second pass. Ask which energy feels most present and which feels most absent. Do not interpret for them. Let them make the links.

Close with one small action that honors both lenses. It should serve the ikigai gap and be carried by an energy the client can actually access this week. If they are depleted, the action might be rest. If they are restless, the action might be a conversation. The point is to keep it small, specific, and kind.
